## The Illusion of Life and Death: A Medical Student's Ordeal 
Huh Kyung young began his lecture by recounting a poignant story of a medical student's first experience with human dissection. The student was tasked with dissecting the body of a young woman who had recently died in a traffic accident. The body was still warm, leading the student to a profound sense of confusion regarding the distinction between life and death. As the professor began the incision, the student experienced a vivid hallucination, believing the woman was about to rise, making it impossible for him to proceed. This visceral reaction highlighted the student's inability to discern between a corpse and a living person, despite the clear pronouncements of death. 

The dissection continued, revealing the woman's internal organs, including a fetus within her womb. This experience deeply traumatized the student, leading to a realization of the intricate complexity of the human body. He marveled at how eleven organs could be meticulously arranged within the confined space of the ribcage, a volume that would otherwise fill a large basin if laid out. This intricate design, he noted, was a testament to a profound, almost magical, creation. The student's subsequent struggles in relationships, often seeing his wife as a mere corpse, underscored the lasting psychological impact of this experience. This narrative served as a powerful introduction to the lecture's core theme: the human tendency to differentiate and discriminate, even when faced with fundamental similarities. 

Huh Kyung young then posed a fundamental question: What truly distinguishes life from death? He explained that from the student's perspective, the presence or absence of a soul was the sole determinant. Without the soul, the body is merely an inanimate object. This led to a broader discussion on the nature of beauty and attraction. The medical student, reflecting on his experience, questioned the societal perception of female beauty, realizing that men are often "bewitched" by appearances. This introspection suggested that what is perceived as beautiful might not be inherently so, but rather a product of human perception and differentiation. 


## Understanding Discrimination and Discernment 
Huh Kyung young then delved into the core concepts of discrimination (차별, <em>chabyeol</em>) and discernment (분별, <em>bunbyeol</em>). He clarified that discrimination refers to differences in quality or quantity. For instance, he used the analogy of alcohol: a "three-distillation" liquor, historically reserved for kings, is qualitatively superior to a "single-distillation" liquor. This qualitative difference constitutes discrimination. Similarly, the value of an object can change based on its environment; a refrigerator, for example, is less valued in an Eskimo village but highly prized in Africa. Gender differences, he noted, also fall under qualitative discrimination. 

Discernment, on the other hand, involves perceiving differences where none fundamentally exist. Huh Kyung young illustrated this with the analogy of water and waves. Both are essentially the same substance, water, yet we discern them as distinct entities: "water" and "wave." The water represents our mind, while the waves symbolize our desires. When the mind is clear, it is like still water, but when desires arise, it becomes turbulent, like waves, obscuring clarity and potentially causing harm. This act of labeling the same substance differently—as water or wave—is discernment, not discrimination. 


## The Cycle of Fortune and Misfortune (새옹지마, *Saeongjima*) 
Huh Kyung young introduced the concept of <em>Saeongjima</em>, a Chinese idiom illustrating the unpredictable nature of fortune and misfortune. He emphasized that good fortune (길, <em>gil</em>) invariably leads to misfortune (흉, <em>hyung</em>), and vice versa. He cited the example of a lottery winner who, despite initial joy, often loses friends, family, and their job, eventually becoming destitute. Conversely, a student who fails university entrance exams might later become a successful entrepreneur, while a student who succeeds might become involved in protests and face ruin. This cyclical nature means that one should not despair over misfortune or become overly elated by good fortune. 

He further elaborated on <em>Saeongjima</em> with the classic tale of the old man whose horse ran away, only to return with a wild mare, bringing both joy and subsequent sorrow when his son broke his leg riding the new horse. However, this injury ultimately saved his son from conscription during a war, allowing him to marry and have many children, while all the other young men in the village perished. This narrative powerfully demonstrates that what appears to be a misfortune can turn into a blessing, and vice versa. The old man's initial despair over his son's injury transformed into gratitude when his son was spared from war. 


## The Three Pillars of Thought: Buddhism, Christianity, and Confucianism 
Huh Kyung young then explored the philosophical underpinnings of three major traditions: Buddhism, Christianity, and Confucianism, and their differing perspectives on karma and suffering. He noted that Confucianism attributes misfortune to ancestral karma, while Buddhism emphasizes individual karma. Christianity, however, attributes good fortune to God's will. 

Buddhism, he explained, can be summarized by the concept of "self-illumination" (자명, <em>jamyeong</em>), which emphasizes emptying the mind and achieving stillness (정, <em>jeong</em>). This passive approach aims to eliminate worries and attract blessings. In contrast, Christianity is characterized by "love" (애, <em>ae</em>) and "action" (동, <em>dong</em>). Christians are encouraged to actively spread their faith, help the needy, and practice love through dynamic engagement with the world. 

Huh Kyung young then questioned whether the old man in the <em>Saeongjima</em> story, a quiet and contemplative figure typical of traditional elders, embodied the Buddhist ideal of stillness. He observed that many elders, despite their quiet demeanor, did not necessarily receive blessings, and their families often faced ruin. This led him to critique the Confucian emphasis on "propriety" (예, <em>ye</em>), which involves strict adherence to rituals like ancestral rites. He questioned whether the misfortunes faced by families adhering to these traditions were a result of their actions or something else entirely. 


## The Nature of Sin and Collective Karma (동업중생, *Dong-eop Jungsaeng*) 
Huh Kyung young presented a thought experiment: a bird catcher uses a caged bird's cries to attract other birds into a net. The caged bird, though forced to cry out, feels immense guilt for the deaths of its companions. Huh Kyung young argued that this bird is not truly sinful because its actions are involuntary. True sin, he explained, arises from a conscious desire to do wrong. If one is forced to commit an act against their will, the sin is not theirs. 

He then introduced the concept of "collective karma" (동업중생, <em>dong-eop jungsaeng</em>), which posits that individuals can share in the karma of others. He challenged the notion that all 500 passengers on a plane that crashes share the same individual karma for death. Instead, he suggested that the pilot's personal karma, perhaps stemming from a domestic dispute and suicidal thoughts, could become the collective karma of all on board. This means that misfortune can befall individuals not due to their own past actions, but due to the actions of another. 


## Overcoming Discrimination and Discernment: The Eight Truths 
Huh Kyung young asserted that understanding collective karma helps eliminate discrimination and discernment. He proposed that misfortune in one area implies happiness in another, and vice versa. This perspective encourages generosity and empathy, as those with abundance should share with the less fortunate, and those experiencing hardship can find solace in the thought that others are benefiting. This understanding, he argued, leads to a state of contentment and freedom from cosmic dissatisfaction. 

He then outlined "Eight Truths" (팔고, <em>palgo</em>) that, when viewed without discernment, prevent suffering. These include: truth and falsehood, good and evil, beauty and ugliness, love and hatred, happiness and unhappiness, praise and blame, existence and non-existence, and gain and loss. He emphasized that perceiving these as distinct and separate leads to stress and negative hormonal responses. For example, while pursuing beauty is natural, one must transcend the distinction between beauty and ugliness to avoid suffering. Similarly, love and hatred, though seemingly opposite, are fundamentally linked in the human heart. Excessive attachment in love, for instance, can lead to the pain of separation. 

Huh Kyung young further explained that true understanding requires viewing things from a "divine perspective" (신관, <em>shingwan</em>), which transcends the limited, two-dimensional human perception. This divine perspective sees the "center" (중심, <em>jungsim</em>) of all things as unified with the divine. He advised that when making decisions, such as purchasing an item or planning a trip, one should consider these eight aspects to avoid the "sins" of discrimination and discernment. 


## The Injustice of Suffering and the Dignity of the Disabled 
Huh Kyung young reiterated that suffering does not always equate to personal sin. He used the example of the 30 families in the <em>Saeongjima</em> story, who, despite being good people, experienced hardship. He also challenged the common misconception that disability is a punishment for past sins. He cited the example of a pilot whose personal karma led to a plane crash, resulting in many deaths and one survivor with a disability. In this scenario, the disability is a consequence of another's actions, not the individual's own sin. Therefore, blaming or shaming disabled individuals is unjust. 

He highlighted the contrasting attitudes towards disabled people in Western societies, particularly Sweden and the Netherlands, where they are highly respected and provided with comprehensive support and employment opportunities. This is rooted in a Christian belief that disabled individuals are sent by God to test the compassion of able-bodied people. This perspective fosters a culture of respect and inclusion from a young age. In contrast, he noted that in Korea, a misinterpretation of Buddhist karma often leads to the stigmatization and bullying of disabled children. 

Huh Kyung young cited the example of Franklin D. Roosevelt, who, despite his disability, served four terms as U.S. President, a testament to the American public's respect and empathy. He concluded by emphasizing that disabled individuals are fundamentally the same as anyone else, just as different cups hold the same wine. Therefore, discrimination against them is unacceptable and must be avoided. 


## The Eight Sufferings (팔고, *Palgo*) Caused by Discrimination 
Huh Kyung young then detailed the "Eight Sufferings" (팔고, <em>palgo</em>) that arise from discrimination and discernment. Avoiding these mental traps, he argued, prevents these sufferings. 

1. **Separation from Loved Ones (****애별이고****, ****aebyeoligo****)**: This suffering arises from the discrimination inherent in love. When one loves someone, they implicitly discriminate against others. This attachment inevitably leads to the pain of separation. 
2. **Encountering Hated Ones (****원증회고****, ****wonjeunghoego****)**: This suffering occurs when one's enemies or those they despise are inevitably encountered, often in unexpected circumstances. 
3. **Unfulfilled Desires (****구부득고****, ****gubudeukgo****)**: This suffering stems from the inability to obtain what one excessively desires. 
4. **The Five Aggregates of Suffering (****오온****성고, ****oonseonggo****)**: This refers to the suffering inherent in the five aggregates of existence (form, sensation, perception, mental formations, consciousness). Discrimination, such as judging food as "delicious" or "tasteless," can lead to health problems like diabetes. This suffering encompasses the inevitable decline of the body through aging, illness, and death. 


## The Power of Huh Kyung young's Name and the Importance of Sustenance 
Huh Kyung young asserted that those who call his name will be spared the suffering of illness and death. He claimed that while all other names in the world deplete energy, his name uniquely boosts the immune system by a factor of 100. He warned of a future pandemic, far more devastating than previous ones, for which conventional medicine will be ineffective. In such a crisis, only calling his name will provide a solution. 

However, he clarified that calling his name is not a substitute for basic sustenance. One must still eat and take nutritional supplements to maintain physical health. His name provides "spiritual protection" (영적인 보호, <em>yeongjeokin boho</em>), enhancing immunity and overall well-being, but it does not provide physical nourishment. He emphasized the importance of eating three meals a day while calling his name for optimal results. 


## The Meaning of the Character for Death (死, *Sa*) 
Huh Kyung young then delved into the etymology of the Chinese character for "death" (死, <em>sa</em>). He explained that the character is composed of two parts: "dagger" (匕, <em>bi</em>) and "spoon" (匙, <em>si</em>). The "dagger" aspect refers to taking a life, while the "spoon" aspect signifies putting down one's spoon at night. He interpreted this to mean that a person who stops eating dinner will eventually die. In ancient times, without modern medical interventions like IV drips, a person who stopped eating would quickly perish. Thus, the act of putting down one's spoon at night symbolized the onset of death. 


## Spiritual Healing and the Role of Angels 
Huh Kyung young demonstrated his spiritual healing abilities by performing a diagnostic and healing session on a volunteer. He claimed to be able to spiritually diagnose and heal various ailments, including rhinitis, liver problems, and brain blockages, simply by looking at the person. He performed a "strength test" where he would press on different parts of the volunteer's body, and the volunteer's ability to resist his pull would indicate the health of that organ. He claimed to instantly heal these conditions, after which the volunteer's strength would return to normal in the affected area. 

He also assessed the volunteer's overall constitution, noting their good health and strong character, attributing their presence at the lecture to their positive human nature. He then performed a similar healing on the volunteer's spine and prostate, claiming to instantly rectify these issues. He explained that these healings are temporary for newcomers, as they are accompanied by the "injection" of angels into the body. He stated that approximately 20 angels are typically deployed. However, these angels will depart if the individual engages in negative behaviors, such as embracing another man's wife or speaking ill of others. The spiritual benefits, he stressed, are contingent upon maintaining a pure heart. 


## The Root Cause of Cancer and the Three Mouths 
Huh Kyung young concluded by addressing the cause of cancer, stating that it arises from selfishness, specifically from having "three mouths" (입이 세 개, <em>ibi se gae</em>). He explained that while a single mouth is sufficient, and even half a mouth would be ideal in the modern era where physical labor is less common, having "three mouths" signifies excessive consumption and self-centeredness. He metaphorically described this as having "mountains" of desires. He further elaborated that what enters the mouth (food) is inherently good, but what comes out of the mouth (words, waste) can be harmful or evil. This distinction highlights the importance of mindful consumption and expression.
