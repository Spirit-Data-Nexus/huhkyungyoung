## Content


## The Principle of Interspace 
Huh Kyung young explains that the concept of "interspace" (사이공간) is fundamental to understanding various phenomena, from the properties of rice to human relationships and societal structures. He illustrates this with the example of rice. Vietnamese rice, known as "alangmi," does not stick together when cooked; it remains separate and airy. This is because there is sufficient space between the individual grains. In contrast, if there is no space between rice grains, it becomes "tteok" (rice cake). In hot climates like Vietnam, rice that lacks this interspace spoils quickly. However, alangmi, with its inherent space, resists spoilage even in high temperatures. This principle extends beyond food: the presence of space prevents decay and corruption in all aspects of life, including marital relationships and corporate environments.


## Fermentation and the Absence of Space 
Fermentation, according to Huh Kyung young, is the process of eliminating space. When rice is mixed with other ingredients and fermented, the spaces between them disappear. This absence of space allows microorganisms to thrive, leading to fermentation, which can also be considered a form of controlled decay. The goal of fermentation is to prevent harmful spoilage by promoting beneficial microbial activity. Thus, the elimination of space is central to both fermentation and, paradoxically, the prevention of undesirable decay.


## Interspace in Marital and Social Relationships 
The concept of interspace is crucial for healthy relationships. Huh Kyung young argues that if there is no space between a husband and wife, conflict is inevitable. The traditional notion of "bubu ilsim dongche" (husband and wife as one body) is, in his view, a misconception that often leads to divorce. Instead, he emphasizes "bubu yubyeol" (husband and wife are distinct), meaning there must always be a space between them. This space signifies mutual respect, where the woman is respected as a woman and the man as a man, without one sacrificing for the other. He observes that couples who walk hand-in-hand, without sufficient space, are not exhibiting a healthy dynamic. He cites former President Park Chung-hee and First Lady Yuk Young-soo as an example of a couple who maintained a respectful distance, which he attributes to a strong and enduring relationship.


## Chulsaegan and Cheosaegan: Two Approaches to Life 
Huh Kyung young distinguishes between two types of individuals: "Chulsaegan" (출세간) and "Cheosaegan" (처세간). Chulsaegan refers to those who transcend worldly affairs, such as monks, priests, or pastors, who dedicate their lives to spiritual pursuits rather than material gain. They are seen as having "left the world." Conversely, Cheosaegan refers to those who live within the world, engaging in business, careers, and everyday life. He clarifies that "Chulsae" (출세), often translated as "success" or "getting ahead," actually means "emerging from the world" in this context. He even suggests that a president, while seemingly powerful, is a "servant" rather than a "Chulsaegan" in the traditional sense, as their role is one of service. He further explains that "In-gan" (인간), meaning "human," literally translates to "between human and human" or "between human and beast," implying that humans exist in a state between being fully human and being beastly. Chulsaegan are those who have achieved a complete human state.


## The Importance of the Middle Path 
Neither Chulsaegan nor Cheosaegan is inherently superior; both have their challenges. The ideal lies in finding a middle path, a balance between these two extremes. This means that individuals in the worldly realm (Cheosaegan) should understand and respect those who have transcended it (Chulsaegan), and vice versa. Conflict arises when this interspace, this understanding of "the middle," is not acknowledged. For instance, a husband focused solely on worldly success and a wife solely on spiritual matters would lead to discord. Huh Kyung young uses the analogy of a lotus flower, which, despite its beauty, grows from mud (Cheosaegan), illustrating that spiritual truth (Chulsaegan) cannot exist without the worldly foundation. He also points out that even a great leader like a president (Chulsaegan) relies on the populace (Cheosaegan).


## The Spirit of Self-Sacrifice: Lee Sun-sin and Park Chung-hee 
Huh Kyung young identifies historical figures like Admiral Lee Sun-sin and President Park Chung-hee as examples of "Chulsaegan" who embodied extreme self-sacrifice. Their defining characteristic was a willingness to die for a greater cause, demonstrating "salsin seongin" (살신성인), or sacrificing oneself for righteousness. He recounts an anecdote where First Lady Yuk Young-soo, on the morning of Park Chung-hee's coup, wisely advised him to look at their sleeping children before leaving, not to caution him against danger, but to instill a sense of responsibility for their future and the nation. This subtle yet powerful message reinforced his resolve.

Lee Sun-sin, similarly, desired to die in battle to avoid the political persecution and potential annihilation of his family (samjok) that awaited him if he survived. Both leaders prioritized the well-being of future generations and the nation's pride over their own lives. Park Chung-hee, for example, used the reparations from Japan not for immediate political gain or personal enrichment, but to build infrastructure and industries like Pohang Iron and Steel for the future prosperity of the nation, even if it meant his generation had to endure hardship. This contrasts sharply with leaders in other developing countries who often squandered such funds on personal luxuries.


## The Role of Women in Maintaining Interspace and Harmony 
Huh Kyung young emphasizes that Chulsaegan individuals, like the righteous monks who fought during the Imjin War, face death without fear because they have no worldly attachments. However, for ordinary people to succeed, they need both the Chulsaegan spirit of dedication and the Cheosaegan ability to navigate reality, such as providing for one's family. The key is to find a balance, a "middle ground," between these two extremes. This balance, or "gong" (공), which means "emptiness" or "space," is what Buddha taught.

He specifically highlights the crucial role of women in maintaining interspace and harmony within families and society. He explains that the Chinese character for "husband" (夫) depicts a man above the heavens, signifying a divine role, while the character for "wife" (婦) shows a woman holding a broom, symbolizing her role in maintaining the household. These distinct roles necessitate space between them. He argues that the notion of "bubu ilsim dongche" (husband and wife as one body) is flawed because it attempts to eliminate this necessary space, leading to conflict.

He further asserts that women are primarily responsible for fostering harmony or discord in relationships. He points out that the Chinese characters for "jealousy" (嫉妬) both contain the radical for "woman," suggesting that jealousy, often stemming from foolish or "sick" women, is a major cause of relational breakdown. Therefore, women who possess wisdom and a broad mind are essential for the success of families and society. They are the ones who can prevent conflict by understanding and respecting the "space" between family members, especially during gatherings like holidays.


## Unity, Fusion, and Harmony: Different Forms of Connection 
Huh Kyung young differentiates between "unity" (통일, unity), "integration" (통합, union/uniformity), "fusion" (융합, fusion), and "harmony" (화합). He explains that "unity" (통일) involves the elimination of space. He then discusses the three elements of "integration" (통합): "yeonhap" (연합, alliance), "gyeolhap" (결합, combination), and "jiphap" (집합, assembly). These still maintain space between their components. For example, states in a federation are formally integrated but retain their individual identities.

However, "fusion" (융합) is characterized by the complete absence of space, akin to fermentation, mixing, or dissolution. This is a deeper form of merging where individual identities are lost. He contrasts "uniformity" (유니폼티), which describes the formal integration of states like the American federation, with true "fusion." Finally, "harmony" (화합) also involves maintaining space, requiring elements like compromise and communication. Even in family harmony, space between individuals (e.g., father and child) is essential.


## The Universal Principle of Interspace 
The principle of interspace is universal. Huh Kyung young illustrates this with the example of electrical wires: positive and negative wires must have space between them; if they touch, they short-circuit and fail to produce light. Similarly, successful families and businesses are those where husband and wife maintain a balanced relationship, neither one dominating the other, but rather harmonizing their distinct roles.

He also connects this to the concept of "ilche yusimjo" (일체유심조), meaning "everything is created by the mind." He interprets the "mind" here as "space" or "interspace." The way this space is managed determines whether the world thrives in harmony or collapses in chaos. He uses the example of tectonic plates: the Earth's plates float on magma, and the spaces between them are crucial. When these plates collide due to the absence of sufficient space, earthquakes and volcanic eruptions occur, causing destruction. Even at the cellular level, cells maintain distinct spaces. Conflict arises when these spaces are violated.


## Conclusion: The Wisdom of Interspace 
Huh Kyung young concludes by reiterating that "interspace" is not a negative concept. The idea of "bubu ilsim dongche" (husband and wife as one body) is a misunderstanding that leads to marital breakdown. Instead, "ilche dongsim" (all minds as one) is an impossibility. A successful household requires the husband and wife to mutually control and adjust to each other, like the give-and-take between political parties. Heaven and Earth are not one; they have distinct roles, and their existence relies on the space between them. The wisdom of interspace is paramount for success in all aspects of life.
