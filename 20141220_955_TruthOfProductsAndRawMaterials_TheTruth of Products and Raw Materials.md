

## 20141220_955_TruthOfProductsAndRawMaterials_TheTruth of Products and Raw Materials

## Content


## The Essence of Products, Raw Materials, and Truth 
Huh Kyung young elucidates the fundamental relationship between products, raw materials, and truth, using the analogy of a car. A car, as a finished product, is composed of various parts, which represent the raw materials. The product, in this context, symbolizes wisdom, while the raw materials embody knowledge. He emphasizes that understanding the distinction between wisdom and knowledge, and by extension, products and raw materials, is crucial for comprehending truth. Truth itself is likened to a "user manual" for humanity, provided by the heavens. This manual dictates how humans should live and utilize their bodies, contrasting with the common human tendency to misuse their lives. Wisdom, according to Huh Kyung young, originates from within the heart, whereas knowledge is acquired from the external world, through learning and experience.


## The Role of Money and the Concept of Success 
Expanding on the analogy, Huh Kyung young integrates the concept of money. Money is categorized as a raw material, a tool to be utilized for a specific purpose. The purpose for which money is used, whether for personal gain or for the benefit of others, defines its ultimate value. He introduces the concept of "success" (성공, <em>seonggong</em>), breaking it down into two characters: <em>seong</em> (성, to achieve) and <em>gong</em> (공, to contribute or bestow). True success, therefore, is not merely about achieving wealth or power, but about using those achievements to contribute to others. A person who accumulates vast wealth but uses it solely for personal indulgence is deemed a failure, whereas someone like Bill Gates, who donates a significant portion of his wealth, is considered successful because he "bestows" his achievements upon society. This principle extends to all aspects of life, where the ultimate goal of achievement should be contribution.


## The Divine User Manual and Human Responsibility 
Huh Kyung young further elaborates on the "user manual" analogy, stating that the commandments given by heaven serve as humanity's guide, representing truth. He stresses the importance of focusing on one's own actions and contributions to others, rather than being preoccupied with how others treat oneself. He posits that societal problems, even crimes, ultimately stem from the collective actions and inactions of the populace. This perspective encourages an active and responsible approach to life, where individuals recognize their role in shaping the world. By diligently following this divine user manual, anyone can achieve success.


## The True Meaning of *Seonggong* (Success) 
The term <em>seonggong</em> (성공), meaning success, is dissected by Huh Kyung young. <em>Seong</em> (성) represents the process of achievement, while <em>gong</em> (공) signifies the ultimate goal or outcome, which is to bestow or contribute. He argues that the purpose of achieving (earning money, for instance) is to enable contribution. A wealthy individual who hoards their riches is not truly successful because they fail to fulfill the "bestowing" aspect of <em>gong</em>. Conversely, someone who achieves and then contributes, like Bill Gates, is genuinely successful. This concept is further illustrated by the idea of "merit" or "contribution" (<em>gongro</em>, 공로), which is recognized and rewarded, as seen in "national meritorious persons" (<em>gukga yugongja</em>, 국가유공자).


## National Meritorious Persons and the Principle of Contribution 
The concept of a "national meritorious person" (<em>gukga yugongja</em>) is presented as a concrete example of <em>gong</em>. These individuals are recognized for their significant contributions to the nation, not for personal gain. Their sacrifices and service are rewarded with various benefits, including financial support, free access to public facilities, and national burial. This system highlights the societal value placed on selfless contribution. Huh Kyung young contrasts this with modern individualism, lamenting the decline in the number of young people willing to make such sacrifices for the nation. He emphasizes that in the hierarchy of loyalty, the nation often takes precedence over family, illustrating the profound importance of national contribution.


## The Hierarchy of Life and the Purpose of Existence 
Huh Kyung young explains that raw materials are less important than the finished product, and once a product is complete, it must be used according to its "truth" or user manual. He uses the example of Bill Gates's philanthropy to illustrate how immense wealth (a product) should be guided by truth (the user manual) to help the poor and needy. This "user manual" also dictates the order of rescue in emergencies, prioritizing children and women over men, and the elderly over the young, reflecting a divine order. He criticizes those who, despite years of learning, fail to grasp the essence of truth, leading to wasted efforts and unfulfilled lives.


## The Imperfection of Earthly Existence and the Path to Happiness 
Huh Kyung young asserts that wisdom leads to blessings, while knowledge alone can lead to unhappiness. He describes the earthly existence of 7 billion people as inherently incomplete and a "sea of suffering" (<em>gohaeui bada</em>). Humans are born into this world to strive for happiness, but life is a continuous cycle of challenges. Just as one achieves a goal, new difficulties arise, preventing true rest or lasting contentment. This constant struggle, he explains, is why people yearn to contribute and help others, but are often hindered by their own life circumstances. He offers his teachings as a "user manual" to navigate these challenges and achieve a more fulfilling existence.


## The Parable of the Talents and the Nature of Heavenly Rewards 
Drawing from the biblical parable of the talents (Matthew 25:15-30), Huh Kyung young interprets the story in terms of wisdom and knowledge. The servant who buried his single talent represents someone with knowledge but lacking the wisdom to utilize it. The servants who multiplied their talents are wise, actively using their resources. He notes that the master praises both the five-talent and two-talent servants equally, emphasizing that the <em>intent</em> and <em>effort</em> to contribute are more important than the <em>quantity</em> of contribution. This contrasts with another biblical passage (Matthew 20:1-16), where all laborers, regardless of their working hours, receive the same wage, illustrating the non-discriminatory nature of heavenly rewards. Huh Kyung young concludes that heavenly blessings are based on the sincerity of one's heart and the desire to serve, rather than the magnitude of one's achievements.


## The Eight Perspectives (Palgan) and the Stages of Human Development 
Huh Kyung young introduces the "Eight Perspectives" (<em>Palgan</em>, 팔간) as a framework for analyzing situations and making wise decisions. These perspectives involve viewing an object or situation from various angles, including its potential benefits (<em>gwagan</em>, 과간) and drawbacks (<em>bulgwangan</em>, 불과간). He illustrates this with the example of buying a diamond ring for a loved one, urging consideration of both the immediate joy it brings and the potential for fostering materialistic dependency. This comprehensive analysis, he argues, is crucial for transforming knowledge into wisdom.

He then outlines an eight-stage path of human development, starting from "examining things" (<em>gyeongmul</em>, 격물) and culminating in "bringing peace to the world" (<em>pyeongcheonha</em>, 평천하). Most people, he observes, remain stuck in the initial stage of <em>gyeongmul</em>, preoccupied with material possessions and failing to achieve the higher purpose of <em>gong</em> (contribution). Only a small percentage progress to "acquiring knowledge" (<em>chiji</em>, 취지), followed by "making one's will sincere" (<em>seongui</em>, 성의), "rectifying the mind" (<em>jeongsim</em>, 정심), "cultivating oneself" (<em>susin</em>, 수신), "regulating the family" (<em>jega</em>, 제가), "governing the state" (<em>chiguk</em>, 치국), and finally, <em>pyeongcheonha</em>. He asserts that his own mission aligns with the final stage of <em>pyeongcheonha</em>, aiming for global unification.


## The Limitations of Material Creations and the True Nature of Buddha 
Huh Kyung young presents a series of riddles to illustrate the limitations of material creations and the nature of true wisdom. He asks what a product made of wood cannot overcome (fire), what a product made of metal cannot overcome (water, due to rust), and what a product made of earth cannot overcome (a hammer). He criticizes those who possess only knowledge, failing to grasp these deeper truths. He connects this to the Buddhist scriptures, specifically the <em>Palgwan Daejanggyeong</em>, which questions the worship of material Buddha statues that are vulnerable to fire, water, or hammers. The true Buddha, he concludes, resides within one's own heart, in one's wisdom. He positions himself as a guide to help people access this inner wisdom, which he refers to as the "user manual."


## Reincarnation, Prophetic Dreams, and the Celestial Hierarchy 
Huh Kyung young discusses the concept of reincarnation, citing biblical genealogies (e.g., Abraham and David's lineage to Jesus) as evidence that individuals like Jesus are reincarnations of past figures. He explains that the Messiah comes in human form, and the lineage described in Matthew's Gospel supports this idea of cyclical existence. He then delves into the nature of prophetic dreams (<em>taemong</em>, 태몽), particularly those involving mythical creatures like dragons and phoenixes. These dreams, he asserts, are not mere figments of imagination but actual premonitions from heaven, indicating the arrival of a significant soul. He describes a celestial hierarchy, where different levels of beings exist, and souls progress through various stages, much like students advancing through elementary, middle, and high school on Earth. He also touches upon the nature of heavenly sustenance, where beings consume light and energy directly from a celestial sun, eliminating the need for physical food or waste.


## The Hardship of Earthly Life and the Promise of Paradise 
Huh Kyung young contrasts the arduousness of earthly life, particularly for women in traditional roles, with the effortless existence in paradise. He vividly describes the relentless labor of housewives, who spend their entire lives preparing meals and raising children, often without modern conveniences. This "sea of suffering" on Earth, he explains, is a stark contrast to the heavenly realm where such mundane tasks are nonexistent. He emphasizes that Earth is a "bottom-tier" planet, a "primary school" for souls to gain experience. Without these earthly experiences, even paradise would be dull. He assures that in paradise, all memories and relationships can be revisited and enjoyed, free from the suffering and impermanence of Earth.


## The Concepts of Non-Birth, Non-Death, Non-Defilement, and the Middle Way 
Huh Kyung young introduces profound philosophical concepts: non-birth (<em>bulsang</em>, 불생), non-death (<em>bulmyeol</em>, 불멸), non-defilement (<em>bujuong</em>, 부정), and the Middle Way (<em>jungdo</em>, 중도). He explains that human existence is a continuous cycle, a "circle" where there is no true beginning or end. Death is merely a transition, an entry into a realm of light, akin to waking from sleep. Therefore, humans are eternal beings, constantly transforming. He also asserts that there is no inherent defilement or purity in the world, as everything is ultimately created by the divine. What appears dirty is merely a perception. The concept of "non-increase, non-decrease" (<em>bulgam</em>, 불감) further emphasizes the unchanging nature of the divine. Finally, he defines the "Middle Way" as being neither one nor two, neither dirty nor clean, neither increasing nor decreasing, representing a state of perfect balance and neutrality.


## Huh Kyung young's Unique Nature and Healing Abilities 
Huh Kyung young distinguishes himself from ordinary humans, stating that he is neither a "raw material" nor a "finished product" in the conventional sense. He possesses extraordinary abilities, such as instantly healing physical ailments, which he demonstrates through "O-ring tests" on audience members. He explains that his power transcends human capabilities, akin to how a woman who has never seen a dragon can dream of one, indicating a divine intervention. He asserts that his healing powers are not magic but a manifestation of his unique energy and connection to a higher realm. He intentionally limits his demonstrations to avoid being perceived as a mere magician, as his ultimate goal is to become president and guide humanity according to divine principles.


## The Importance of Contribution and the True Meaning of Prayer 
Huh Kyung young reiterates the importance of <em>gong</em> (contribution) and understanding the "user manual" of truth. He emphasizes that human existence is eternal, and individuals can remain on Earth for eons if they fail to grasp these principles. He urges his audience to study his teachings diligently, as they offer a path to becoming "finished products" worthy of ascending to higher realms. He clarifies that prayer (<em>gido</em>, 기도) is not about passively asking for favors, but about active labor and effort. True prayer, he explains, is the diligent work and sweat that enables one to contribute to others. He criticizes the modern tendency to seek shortcuts and instant gratification, asserting that genuine happiness and fulfillment are the result of persistent effort and selfless contribution.
