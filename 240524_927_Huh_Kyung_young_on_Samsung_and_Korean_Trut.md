## Content

Huh Kyung young explains that sound and vibration originate from the same fundamental source. He recounts an anecdote about Samsung's former chairman, Lee Kun-hee, who reportedly fell ill a week after Huh Kyung young mentioned that Samsung would be managed by "Lee Hwa-kyung" (meaning "management by two Lees"). This incident, according to Huh Kyung young, demonstrates the profound influence of sound and intention on human life and destiny. He asserts that while conventional methods like meditation or viewing images can alter one's physical state, his own image or even the thought of him can induce a complete transformation, even if it's as small as a grain of rice. This is because his image generates an infinite vibration that can change the body, a phenomenon he claims is unique to him, as other materials or individuals do not possess such vibrational power.


## The Dual Nature of the Korean Presidency and the Role of the Prime Minister 
Huh Kyung young critiques the structure of the South Korean government, particularly the role of the Prime Minister within a presidential system. He highlights Article 64 of the Korean Constitution, which describes the President as having a dual nature: both the head of state (원수) and the head of government (수반). The head of state represents the nation, while the head of government oversees administrative affairs. In contrast, countries like Japan have an Emperor as the head of state and a Prime Minister as the head of government, and the UK has a Queen and a Prime Minister. Huh Kyung young argues that having a Prime Minister in a presidential system like Korea's is problematic because it dilutes the President's authority and can lead to national instability.


## Critique of the Prime Ministerial System in Korea 
Huh Kyung young contends that the Prime Minister's role in Korea is redundant and detrimental to effective governance. He explains that in a presidential system, the President directly appoints and directs cabinet ministers. The existence of a Prime Minister, who is meant to assist the President in managing cabinet affairs, creates an unnecessary layer of bureaucracy and diffuses accountability. He contrasts this with the American system, where a powerful President directly commands cabinet members without a Prime Minister. Huh Kyung young asserts that in a nation like Korea, which faces constant security threats due to its division, a strong, unified presidential leadership is essential. He dismisses the concept of a "responsible prime ministerial system" as suitable only for parliamentary democracies like Japan, the UK, or Germany, not for Korea.


## The President as the "Engine" and the Prime Minister as the "Tire" 
To illustrate his point, Huh Kyung young uses an analogy: the President is the engine of a car, and the Prime Minister is merely a tire. The engine initiates movement and dictates speed, while the tire simply follows. If the tire encounters rough terrain and bursts, it can hinder the car's progress. He argues that the presence of a Prime Minister allows the President to evade responsibility, as failures can be attributed to the Prime Minister. This, he claims, undermines the President's accountability and leadership. He advocates for the abolition of the Prime Ministerial system in Korea, suggesting that a Vice President could fulfill any necessary supporting roles, as is the case in the United States.


## The Dangers of a Divided Executive and the Need for a Unified Leader 
Huh Kyung young further elaborates on the dangers of a divided executive. He explains that in a true presidential system, the Vice President assumes full presidential powers if the President is incapacitated or dies. However, in Korea, the Prime Minister does not possess such authority, leading to a power vacuum and potential chaos in times of crisis. He criticizes the current system where the Prime Minister is expected to take responsibility for national security or major incidents, while the President remains detached. This, he argues, is akin to having two fathers or two mothers, creating confusion and inefficiency. He believes that the current system, with its emphasis on a Prime Minister, is a "coup d'état" against the presidential system and must be rectified.


## The President's Dual Role and the "Imperial Presidency" 
Huh Kyung young reiterates that the Korean Constitution (Article 64) explicitly grants the President a dual role as both head of state and head of government, effectively making the Korean President an "imperial president" with powers comparable to a monarch. He argues that this constitutional provision makes the existence of a Prime Minister unnecessary and contradictory. He criticizes politicians who advocate for a "responsible prime ministerial system," calling such proposals "empty promises" that lead to national disunity. He emphasizes that the President's dual authority is a fundamental truth of the Korean system, and any attempt to introduce a Prime Minister undermines this inherent structure.


## The Problem of a "Smart" Prime Minister 
Huh Kyung young warns against the appointment of a "smart" or highly capable Prime Minister in a presidential system. He cites the example of former Prime Minister Lee Hae-chan, whose intelligence and assertiveness reportedly clashed with the President's authority, leading to friction and instability. He argues that if a Prime Minister is perceived as more capable than the President, it creates a power struggle that can destabilize the government. In a presidential system, the President should be the ultimate authority, and any subordinate figure, including the Prime Minister, should not challenge that authority. He suggests that the President should directly manage affairs, perhaps with the assistance of a few Vice Prime Ministers, rather than a single, powerful Prime Minister.


## Samsung's Strategic Evolution: From Blue to Red 
Huh Kyung young shifts his focus to Samsung, discussing its global standing and strategic direction. He notes that Samsung's corporate value is immense, contributing significantly to Korea's GDP. He then delves into the symbolism of Samsung's corporate logo color. He explains that the current blue logo represents a cautious and stable approach, characteristic of Lee Kun-hee's leadership. However, to dominate the global market under Lee Jae-yong's leadership, Samsung needs to adopt a more assertive and leading role, symbolized by the color red. Red, he explains, signifies "leadership" and "driving force." He believes that changing the logo to red, while maintaining its core design, would project a new image of global leadership and charisma, signaling a shift from cautious growth to aggressive market dominance.


## The Need for Visionary Leadership in Korea 
Huh Kyung young criticizes the lack of visionary leadership in Korea, particularly in areas like biotechnology. He points out that Samsung, despite its vast capital, has not fully invested in groundbreaking fields like stem cell research, which could be transformative for the nation. He laments that Korea often stifles innovation and dismisses brilliant minds like Hwang Woo-suk, hindering the nation's future progress. He reiterates that Samsung needs to embrace a "red" strategy, signifying bold leadership and innovation, to truly lead the world. He claims that he foresaw these changes years ago and that his insights are now becoming reality, emphasizing the need for a "prophet" or "seer" to guide the nation.


## The Shifting Paradigms of Korean Governance 
Huh Kyung young analyzes the changing political paradigms in Korea, which have shifted from "welfare" to "economic democratization," then to "deregulation," and finally to "safety." He argues that each shift in policy focus creates confusion and inefficiency among government officials, who constantly have to adapt to new directives. He criticizes the "creative economy" initiative, suggesting it merely rehashes existing welfare and economic democratization policies under a new name, primarily focusing on tax collection and wealth redistribution rather than genuine innovation. He believes these constant shifts in national policy demonstrate a lack of consistent vision and long-term planning.


## The Pitfalls of Over-Emphasizing Safety and Deregulation 
Huh Kyung young discusses the unintended consequences of the "deregulation" and "safety" paradigms. He argues that excessive deregulation can lead to national chaos and instability, as seen in various incidents. Conversely, an overemphasis on safety, while seemingly positive, can lead to increased costs for businesses, hindering economic growth. He uses the example of car safety regulations, where adding numerous safety features significantly increases the price of vehicles, ultimately burdening consumers and businesses. He suggests that a balanced approach is necessary, rather than swinging from one extreme to another.


## The Threat of Structural Weaknesses and Geopolitical Instability 
Huh Kyung young highlights critical structural weaknesses in Korea, such as the vulnerability of buildings to earthquakes, particularly in densely populated areas like Gangnam. He points out that many buildings are constructed on sand, making them susceptible to collapse if groundwater is extracted, as seen in the case of the Lotte World Tower. He warns of potential large-scale disasters if these issues are not addressed. He then shifts to geopolitical concerns, noting the growing alliances between Russia, China, and North Korea, juxtaposed with the alliance between the US and Japan. He expresses concern that the Korean Peninsula is becoming a "lonely island" caught between these major powers, potentially leading to its division or exploitation. He recounts historical instances where major powers conspired to divide Korea, emphasizing the need for strong, independent leadership.


## The Cost of the Korean War and the Geopolitical Chessboard 
Huh Kyung young delves into the historical context of the Korean War, highlighting the immense human cost, particularly for China, which lost 700,000 soldiers. He argues that the Soviet Union cleverly manipulated the situation, encouraging North Korea to invade and drawing the US into the conflict. This, he claims, allowed the Soviet Union to achieve its strategic goal of having US forces stationed in South Korea, thereby containing China and creating a buffer against American influence in Asia. He suggests that the Korean War was a complex geopolitical maneuver, with major powers pursuing their own interests at Korea's expense.


## The Arrival of the Messiah and the Three Poisons 
Huh Kyung young asserts that in this era of global instability and complex challenges, Korea needs a "Messiah" – a political, not religious, figure – to unify the world and resolve these issues. He claims to be this Messiah, sent from heaven to bring about global unity. He criticizes the inability of ordinary humans to comprehend and solve these intricate problems, stating that only a divinely appointed leader can achieve such a feat. He then introduces the Buddhist concept of the "Three Poisons" (삼독): greed (탐), anger (진), and ignorance (치). He emphasizes that ignorance (치) is the most dangerous of these, as it leads to all other sins and ultimately causes suffering and national decline.


## The Importance of Wisdom and the Fruits of the Spirit 
Huh Kyung young stresses the paramount importance of wisdom (지혜) in overcoming ignorance. He argues that without wisdom, individuals are prone to making foolish decisions that harm themselves and their nation. He illustrates this with a story of a naive servant who, lacking wisdom, unknowingly poisons his master with arsenic, believing it to be a beneficial powder. This anecdote underscores how a lack of knowledge can lead to catastrophic outcomes, even with good intentions. He then connects this to Christian teachings, referencing the "Fruits of the Spirit" (성령의 열매): love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, goodness, faithfulness, gentleness, and self-control. He claims that those who receive his "spirit" or call his name will embody these virtues and experience transformation.


## The True Meaning of Happiness and the Role of Self-Control 
Huh Kyung young defines true happiness not as fleeting pleasure (쾌락), but as profound joy (희락) that originates from within. He explains that pleasure is a temporary hormonal response, while joy is a deeper, more enduring state that promotes health and well-being. He uses the analogy of a child choosing between playing and studying: playing offers immediate pleasure, but studying, though initially difficult, leads to long-term joy and fulfillment. He emphasizes the importance of self-control (절제) and loyalty (충성) – not just to external figures, but to one's own divine essence and ancestors. He asserts that living by these principles, rather than pursuing superficial pleasures, is the path to true happiness and national prosperity.


## The Legacy of Jang Bogo and the "Cheonghaejin" Incident 
Huh Kyung young concludes by referencing the historical figure Jang Bogo, a powerful maritime commander of the Silla Dynasty who established Cheonghaejin as a major international trading hub. He contrasts Jang Bogo's era of national greatness with the modern "Cheonghaejin" incident, referring to the Sewol ferry disaster. He laments that while Jang Bogo brought glory to Korea, the Sewol incident brought national shame and a negative global image. He suggests that this disaster, occurring in a place historically significant for maritime trade, symbolizes a deeper national crisis. He believes that such events are signs that the time has come for a new leader, a "prophet" or "seer," to emerge and guide Korea through its current challenges, especially as major powers continue to exert influence over the Korean Peninsula.
