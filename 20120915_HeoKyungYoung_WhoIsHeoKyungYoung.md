He ordered his driver to stop the car, then ran 500 meters to the scene.  Alone, he confronted the ten attackers, striking the largest one on both cheeks.  The situation was tense, but Heo Kyung-young remained calm, his expression unchanged.  His powerful strike, combined with an incomprehensible "dialect" he spoke, instantly subdued the attackers, causing nine of them to kneel.  He also demonstrated a supernatural speed, a "chukjibeop" (縮地法, a legendary Korean technique of shortening distance), to reach them instantly.  He then helped the injured man and the woman who was crying in the car to leave for the hospital, making the attackers promise not to repeat their actions before releasing them.

He resolved the situation more swiftly than the police, acting as an immediate arbiter.  He emphasizes that an ordinary person attempting such an intervention would be beaten to death. ### 01:10:00 The True Meaning of Love
Heo Kyung-young redefines love, moving away from abstract religious interpretations. He outlines four practical forms of love:
1. **Ansi (眼施 - Giving with the Eyes):** Expressing joy and kindness through one's facial expressions. 
2. **Eonsi (言施 - Giving with Words):** Speaking gently and kindly to others. 
3. **Mulsi (物施 - Giving with Material Possessions):** Sharing material wealth with others. 
4. **Sinsi (身施 - Giving with the Body):** Offering physical service, such as yielding a seat or volunteering. 

He criticizes traditional religious teachings that advocate for loving enemies or turning the other cheek, calling them impractical and leading to psychological distress.  He argues that true love means not creating situations where others have to endure suffering.  For example, a loving husband should not make his wife endure hardships, but rather resolve issues so she doesn't have to "bear" anything.  He also states that if one truly loves someone, they would wish for that person to find happiness, even if it means being with someone else who can provide a better life. 

### 01:20:00 The Fallacy of Prayer
Heo Kyung-young asserts that prayer, in the sense of asking God for things, is a "sin."  He explains that God has already provided humanity with everything necessary, and therefore, asking for more implies a lack of gratitude or a belief that God is not loving.  He equates such prayer with "kibok" (祈福), or praying for personal blessings, which he sees as a selfish act.  He believes that instead of asking, people should express gratitude and acknowledge their own shortcomings in utilizing the blessings they've already received.  He criticizes the practice of constantly asking for things, even from deceased parents, as a misguided and ineffective approach.  He suggests that such prayers only lead to continued conflict and suffering in society. 

### 01:28:00 The Story of Choi Buja's Rice Jar
Heo Kyung-young shares the story of the "Choi Buja" (Rich Choi) family, who survived the Yeosu-Suncheon Rebellion and the Korean War without harm, despite being wealthy.  He attributes their survival to their consistent acts of charity.  The Choi family had a rice jar with a hole at the bottom, placed outside their house, allowing anyone in need to take rice discreetly at night.  The daughter-in-law was responsible for ensuring the jar was never empty.  This practice ensured that no one in the village went hungry, and the villagers, including those who later became rebels, protected the Choi family during times of conflict.  Heo Kyung-young uses this story to illustrate that consistent, selfless giving, even in small ways, builds immense goodwill and protection.  He concludes by stating that one's face can be a "rice jar," meaning that by maintaining a joyful and kind demeanor, one can accumulate blessings without needing money.  He encourages his audience to spread his teachings as a way of performing this "greatest blessing."
