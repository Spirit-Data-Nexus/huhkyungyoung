## The Truth of Divine Will and Incomprehensibility


## Date: July 26, 2014


## Keywords: Shinmyeong, Incomprehensibility, Korean Politics, Geopolitics, Spiritual Consciousness


## Content


## The Essence of Shinmyeong and Societal Decline 
Huh Kyung young emphasizes the critical importance of "Shinmyeong" (divine will or spiritedness) in all aspects of life, from personal endeavors to national governance. He notes that without Shinmyeong, individuals cannot succeed, and their children will also struggle. This spiritedness is characterized by a keen intellect and a vibrant energy in one's eyes, signifying a clear and bright mind. He observes a pervasive lack of Shinmyeong in contemporary Korean society, affecting politicians, public officials, and even merchants, leading to widespread dissatisfaction and a sense of hopelessness. 

This decline is evident in various societal phenomena: students are disengaged and sleepy in classrooms, a stark contrast to historical examples of diligent study. Businesses struggle, with owners feeling trapped in cycles of debt and merely covering expenses. Family life is also impacted, with a noticeable absence of joy and laughter in homes, contributing to rising divorce rates and young people's inability to marry due to financial burdens. Huh Kyung young attributes these issues to a lack of Shinmyeong-infused leadership and policies that fail to uplift the populace. 

He asserts his unique ability to restore Shinmyeong, claiming that his presence alone brings joy and relief to people, momentarily alleviating their suffering. He encourages his audience to share his message, suggesting that bringing others to hear his lectures is a way to accumulate blessings. He humorously references the proverb "A dog at a village school recites poetry after three years" (서당 개 삼 년에 풍월을 넘는다), implying that even casual exposure to his teachings can lead to profound understanding. 


## Reinterpreting Ancient Wisdom and Societal Ills 
Huh Kyung young challenges conventional interpretations of classical phrases and concepts, arguing that much of what is commonly accepted as truth is fundamentally flawed. He critiques the traditional understanding of "토사구팽" (tosa-gupaeng), which means "when the hares are caught, the hounds are cooked." He argues that the character "구" (gu) refers to a small dog or puppy, which would be incapable of catching a hare, suggesting that the correct character should be "견" (gyeon), meaning a larger, hunting dog. This reinterpretation highlights a broader issue of societal misunderstanding and the perpetuation of incorrect knowledge. 

He extends this critique to the very structure of the Korean language and its relationship with Chinese characters. He claims that words like "얼굴" (eolgul, face) and "사랑" (sarang, love) lack corresponding Chinese characters in traditional dictionaries because the compilers were not "geniuses" like himself. He offers his own etymological breakdown of "얼굴" as "그루터기" (geurutogi, stump) and "굴" (gul, cave), metaphorically representing the brain as a cave containing the "stump" of one's essence. For "사랑," he proposes it signifies "four joys": giving joy to others through one's face, words, material possessions, and physical service. 

He further dissects the Chinese character for "명" (myeong, life/fate), explaining that it implies the inability to speak "two words with one mouth," connecting it to the necessity of food and air for survival. He argues that every character holds a profound meaning that is often overlooked or misinterpreted by common understanding. He reiterates that these misinterpretations, like the "tosa-gupaeng" example, reflect a broader societal blindness to truth, which he, Huh Kyung young, is uniquely positioned to correct. 


## Geopolitical Strategies and the Korean Peninsula's Predicament 
Huh Kyung young delves into geopolitical analysis, asserting that current political leaders are akin to "puppies trying to catch tigers," incapable of addressing complex international challenges. He uses the "tosa-gupaeng" analogy to describe how powerful nations exploit weaker ones. He cites the Vietnam War as an example, where the U.S. allegedly used South Vietnam to defeat North Vietnam, only to then turn North Vietnam against China and the Soviet Union, ultimately serving American interests. He warns that South Korea is next on this "tosa-gupaeng" list. 

He then provides a historical account of the U.S. military base in Yongsan, Seoul, tracing its origins not to Japanese aggression, but to a conflict between King Gojong and his father, Daewongun. He explains that a military rebellion, sparked by unpaid and adulterated wages, led Gojong to seek assistance from Qing China, who then established a military presence in Yongsan. This, he argues, was an act of "tosa-gupaeng" by Gojong against his own father, highlighting the impure origins of foreign military presence in Korea. 

Huh Kyung young suggests that the U.S. desire to move its military base to Pyeongtaek is part of a larger strategy to unify the Korean Peninsula under North Korean leadership, thereby creating a direct border with China and Russia. This, he claims, would allow Japan to control the peninsula, serving American and Japanese interests. He warns that Korea is being manipulated into a "tosa-gupaeng" scenario, where its allies will ultimately betray it. He asserts that he alone possesses the spiritual foresight to prevent a third world war, which he believes is imminent without his intervention. 


## The Six-Day War and the Power of Incomprehensibility (Busa-ui) 
Huh Kyung young recounts the 1967 Six-Day War between Israel and a coalition of Arab nations, highlighting the seemingly miraculous Israeli victory against overwhelming odds. He notes that Israel, with a population of 3 million and only 30,000 soldiers, defeated an Arab coalition of 2.5 million troops in just six days. He attributes this victory to a "secret weapon" provided by the U.S., which caused mass panic and flight among the Arab forces. He describes this weapon as a chemical agent that instantly killed people, leading to "혼비백산" (honbi-baeksan), meaning "souls flying away and spirits scattering." 

He explains that the Arab nations, with a combined population of 1.1 billion, were utterly defeated by a much smaller Israel, which he attributes to advanced weaponry. He frames this conflict as a religious war between Islam and Judaism, arguing that such religious conflicts will ultimately lead to a third world war, as prophesied in various scriptures. He states that his purpose in coming to Earth is to prevent this global catastrophe, claiming that no one else, not even world leaders like Ban Ki-moon or the U.S. President, can stop these conflicts. 

He criticizes the "educated" people of the world, including Harvard graduates, for their inability to grasp the true nature of these threats, likening them to "garbage" lacking Shinmyeong. He warns of the constant danger of terrorism, where everyday objects can be transformed into bombs, making global travel unsafe. He compares the current global situation to children playing with dynamite next to a stove, emphasizing the precariousness of the world without proper leadership. He asserts that without a leader who can inspire Shinmyeong, all human achievements are built on sand, destined to collapse. 


## The Three Souls and Seven Spirits (Sam-hon Chil-baek) 
Huh Kyung young introduces the concept of "Sam-hon Chil-baek" (three souls and seven spirits), which he equates with the state of "혼비백산" (honbi-baeksan) or death. He explains that the human body contains three souls (심혼 - heart soul, 정혼 - essence soul, 영혼 - spirit soul) and seven spirits, which he identifies with the seven orifices of the face: two eyes, two ears, two nostrils, and one mouth. These souls and spirits work in harmony to sustain life. 

He describes what happens when these souls and spirits depart: upon death, the body begins to decompose, and maggots emerge, which were previously held in check by the Sam-hon Chil-baek. He explains that the three souls depart simultaneously, followed by the seven spirits in a specific order. Doctors can check for the presence of these spirits by examining the red lines under the eyelids, which indicate whether a person is merely unconscious or truly deceased. He uses the example of drowning victims, where checking these lines can determine if resuscitation is possible. 

He details the sequential departure of the seven spirits as a person approaches death: first, the sense of taste and the ability to eat are lost (mouth); then, hearing fades (ears); next, sight diminishes (eyes); and finally, the ability to breathe ceases (nose). He states that doctors confirm death not just by the cessation of the heartbeat, but by the absence of these souls and spirits. He reiterates that the Israeli victory in the Six-Day War was a result of the Arab forces experiencing "혼비백산," a state of complete spiritual and physical disarray, which he attributes to the incomprehensible power of the divine. 


## Religious Conflict and the Korean Peninsula's Destiny 
Huh Kyung young explains that the Israeli victory in the Six-Day War was attributed by Israelis to the superiority of their God, Yahweh, over the God of Islam, further fueling religious tensions. He argues that this religious pride makes the conflict endless. He asserts that if he were president, he would spiritually resolve such conflicts. 

He warns that the Korean Peninsula is currently caught in a "tosa-gupaeng" strategy orchestrated by the U.S. and Japan. He claims that the U.S. is secretly releasing North Korean funds through Japan to incite conflict, aiming to destabilize the peninsula and create a direct border with China and Russia, thereby allowing Japan to control Korea. He states that he can see these hidden geopolitical maneuvers, emphasizing that relying on the U.S. for security is a mistake, as they will ultimately betray Korea, just as they did Vietnam. He believes that a crucial figure like himself, Huh Kyung young, is needed to guide Korea through these treacherous times and establish a peaceful world. 


## The Incomprehensible (Busa-ui) and Spiritual Consciousness 
Huh Kyung young introduces the concept of "Busa-ui" (incomprehensibility), which he defines as a miracle or a spiritual perception beyond the five senses (육식 - six consciousnesses, referring to the five senses plus mental consciousness). He contrasts this with "사유" (sayu, thought/reason), which is limited to ordinary human perception. He explains that while ordinary people perceive the world through their five senses, Busa-ui allows for "영감" (yeonggam, spiritual intuition or inspiration), a higher form of perception. 

He illustrates Busa-ui with an anecdote about Samsung's "Mach Management" (마하 경영). He claims that when a professor asked him about the meaning of this term, he immediately knew it signified an impending collapse, like a heart attack, for Samsung's leadership. He contrasts this with the inability of a Harvard professor to interpret the same term, attributing it to their reliance on ordinary, limited perception. He argues that "Mach Management," signifying something infinitely large, actually foreshadowed the downfall of Samsung's leader, Lee Jae-yong. This, he says, demonstrates the power of Busa-ui to see beyond the obvious and predict future events. 


## Economic Policies and the Vision of a Utopian Society 
Huh Kyung young criticizes current economic policies, such as the government's 41 trillion won quantitative easing, arguing that such measures only benefit the wealthy and fail to stimulate the economy or help those in need. He cites a past instance where 45 trillion won allocated to farmers ended up being used for gambling rather than agricultural development, leading to farmers accumulating debt and losing their land to banks. He asserts that these policies ultimately impoverish the common people. 

He proposes an alternative: a "New Deal" program that would employ 5 million unemployed citizens in agricultural and herbal medicine initiatives. This would involve cultivating ginseng and medicinal herbs in mountains, eliminating pesticide factories, and transforming all rice production into eco-friendly farming. He envisions a scenario where Korean organic rice would command premium prices globally, making it immune to market fluctuations caused by rice imports. 

His New Deal would involve converting small fields into large, mechanized farms and establishing "Utopia Districts" where individuals, from 15 to 100 years old, could work and live. Each person would receive 1 million won per month, with couples earning 2 million won. These districts would provide food and lodging, allowing people to move freely between different locations (e.g., Chuncheon, Seoraksan, Jeju Island) while continuing to earn. He believes this system would foster patriotism, strengthen family bonds, and eliminate marital conflicts by providing financial independence and a supportive community environment. He argues that such a system, based on Busa-ui, would truly revitalize the economy and create a utopian society, unlike the current policies that only benefit a select few. 


## The Divine Consciousness and the Role of Huh Kyung young 
Huh Kyung young reiterates that perceiving the world through the "six consciousnesses" (육식) prevents one from achieving a bright, divine spirit. He then discusses the structure of religious texts, mentioning the five books of Moses (Genesis, Exodus, Leviticus, Numbers, Deuteronomy) and the New Testament, implying a deeper, spiritual understanding of these scriptures. He suggests that the ongoing conflicts in the Middle East, driven by religious factions, are leading to a third world war, and similar tensions exist on the Korean Peninsula, surrounded by powerful nations like Japan, Russia, and China, all vying for strategic control. 

He describes the Korean Peninsula as a critical "triangular point" where the interests of China, Russia, and North Korea converge, with Japan also playing a role. He claims that the U.S. seeks to control this strategic point, but cannot do so without first "eating" South Korea and Japan. He asserts that he, Huh Kyung young, has come to resolve these complex geopolitical issues, particularly those on the Korean Peninsula, which he finds less challenging than the Middle East conflicts. He views the conflicts involving Iran and other nations as insignificant in his spiritual sight. 


## Korea's Unique Spiritual Destiny and the Power of Busa-ui 
Huh Kyung young explains Korea's unique spiritual destiny by referencing biblical dietary laws. He states that while other powerful nations like the U.S. (turtle), China (eagle), and Russia (elephant) are "animals" with cloven hooves (representing duality), Korea is like an animal with a single hoof. In the Bible, animals with single hooves are forbidden to eat, signifying that Korea is a nation that cannot be "eaten" or conquered by others. He cites historical examples of Japan, Russia, and China attempting to conquer Korea and ultimately failing or suffering severe consequences. 

He asserts that his presence in Korea is precisely because it is a "forbidden" land, a place that cannot be dominated by other nations. He then connects this to the concept of "Busa-ui" (incomprehensibility) through the phenomenon of Buddhist relics (sari) appearing in pagodas. He explains that these relics often appear through "spatial transfer" rather than physical transportation, a miraculous event that ordinary people cannot comprehend. He states that monks build pagodas and perform diligent prayers, and then, through an inexplicable phenomenon, the relics appear inside. This, he says, is Busa-ui, a miracle that also occurs in churches and cathedrals, though sari are its most representative form. He adds that if a temple's abbot engages in corrupt practices, the sari can disappear, a fact known to all Buddhist practitioners. 


## The Energy of Huh Kyung young's Name and the Coming Paradise 
Huh Kyung young claims that his name possesses the power of "Busa-ui," allowing him to heal illnesses, change bodies, and instill strength and vitality simply by being invoked. He contrasts this with the Pope, who, despite his religious authority, does not possess such energy in his name. He recounts an instance where Pope Francis expressed discomfort when people called his name instead of Jesus, acknowledging that his name lacks the divine energy to bring about miracles. 

He asserts that while all other names on Earth lack Busa-ui, his name, Huh Kyung young, is imbued with it. He claims that when people invoke his name, they experience Shinmyeong (divine will/spiritedness), and their spirits brighten. He states that approximately 200 million people worldwide already experience this joy and spiritedness by calling his name. He prophesies that if he becomes president, not only Korea but the entire world will transform into a paradise, filled with Shinmyeong. 

He acknowledges that his initial appearance might seem strange to some, but believes they will eventually understand. He uses the metaphor of a dragon ascending to the heavens, accompanied by clouds, fog, thunder, and darkness, to describe the current political climate. He suggests that the world is becoming darker and more chaotic, creating the necessary conditions for his emergence as a leader. He states that he will appear when society is at its lowest point, when people are desperate for change. 

He then delves into a more complex spiritual framework, distinguishing between the six consciousnesses (육식), the seventh consciousness (말라야식 - Malaya-vijnana, the defiled mind), the eighth consciousness (아뢰야식 - Alaya-vijnana, the storehouse consciousness, representing past lives), and the ninth consciousness (신의식 - divine consciousness). He explains that his ability to heal and bring about change stems from this ninth, infinite, divine consciousness, which transcends the unconscious and subconscious realms. He recounts a recent visit to an English academy where his presence, singing, and interactions brought immense joy to students, parents, and even American attendees, demonstrating the widespread impact of his divine energy. He notes that people instinctively seek to take photos with him first, recognizing the spiritual significance of his presence. 


## The Truth of Karma, Disability, and Compassion 
Huh Kyung young discusses the concepts of fate, destiny, and past lives, acknowledging that while he transcends these, ordinary people must be mindful of them. He uses the example of a child born with polio to illustrate the differing perspectives of Christianity and Buddhism. Christians, he explains, view such a child as specially chosen by God to bring glory through their disability, leading to societies where disabled individuals are highly valued and supported by the state, with guaranteed employment. This educational approach fosters confidence in disabled individuals and encourages compassion from others. 

In contrast, he states that Buddhist societies often attribute disability to sins from past lives, leading to less supportive welfare systems and a tendency to view disabled individuals with pity or judgment. He argues that while the concept of past life karma might be a "truth," the Christian approach of viewing disability as a means for divine glory is more beneficial for societal education and fostering compassion. He emphasizes that regardless of the underlying truth, people should never judge or speak ill of others, especially the disabled or those who have experienced divorce, as this creates negative karma. 

He concludes by stressing the importance of compassion and service. He encourages people to see those in need as opportunities to do good, to accumulate blessings, and to teach their children empathy and service. He asserts that while certain truths exist, from a divine perspective, such distinctions are not paramount. He warns against superficial understanding, stating that "a half-baked shaman catches people," implying that incomplete knowledge can be harmful. He urges people to protect the weak and those of different faiths, fostering mutual respect and leading to a better collective future, all while invoking his name.
