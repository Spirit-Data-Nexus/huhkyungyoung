## Content


## The Integrity of Nammyung Jo Shik and the Essence of Hwaeomgye 
Nammyung Jo Shik, an ancestor of our assistant professor, was known for his unyielding integrity. He famously rejected King Seonjo's three invitations to serve as Prime Minister, stating that he preferred to live like a turtle in the mudflats, serving as an inspiration to the people, rather than becoming a "stuffed specimen" in the royal court. This steadfastness reflects a profound philosophical stance: that true value lies not in worldly power or recognition, but in maintaining one's principles and contributing to the well-being of the nation from an uncompromised position. This resonates with the concept of "Sasamooae" (四事無碍) from the Hwaeomgye (Avatamsaka Sutra), which signifies a state of being unhindered or unconstrained in all matters.


## Sasamooae and the Modern Predicament 
The Hwaeomgye, a vast scripture translated into 80 volumes, can be condensed into the principle of Sasamooae. This state implies an absence of conflict or obstruction in any interaction. For instance, if one is cut off in traffic, a person who has attained Sasamooae would not engage in conflict, recognizing a fundamental unity with the other person. This is a higher stage than "Isamooae" (二事無碍), which represents a lesser degree of unhinderedness. The modern world, however, often sees individuals, particularly younger generations, easily provoked into conflict over minor disputes. This contrasts sharply with the ideal of Sasamooae, where one remains unperturbed and free from entanglement in any situation.


## Seonggol, Jingol, and the Cheonbugyeong 
The lecture then delves into the Silla Dynasty's bone-rank system, distinguishing between "Seonggol" (聖骨, sacred bone) and "Jingol" (眞骨, true bone). Seonggol refers to individuals whose parents were both of royal lineage, while Jingol refers to those with only one royal parent. The Joseon Dynasty, in contrast, had no Seonggol, only Jingol. Huh Kyung young asserts that from the perspective of the Korean people, existing religions like Christianity and Buddhism, with their foreign origins, are akin to "Jingol" because they represent a mixture of different bloodlines or traditions. He posits that the true "Seonggol" of the Korean people is the Cheonbugyeong, which embodies the pure, unmixed essence of the nation's spiritual heritage. He criticizes those who adhere blindly to foreign religions without understanding their own ancestral roots, likening them to individuals who are "problematic" for failing to preserve their unique heritage.


## Critiques of Catholicism and Protestantism 
Huh Kyung young offers a sharp critique of both Catholicism and Protestantism. He argues that Catholicism has "damaged" religion by creating idols in the form of statues of Jesus within cathedrals, thereby altering core doctrines. He specifically points to the sale of indulgences, which funded the construction of the Vatican, as a "sin against heaven" and a distortion of religious principles. Protestantism, he contends, is characterized by constant accusations of "heresy" against other denominations, leading to its decline. He emphasizes that while he appreciates all religions, he believes they should not be sources of division or power struggles.

## The True Mark of Judaism and the Need for Change 
Huh Kyung young corrects the common misconception about the Jewish symbol, stating that it is not the Star of David but rather the seven-branched menorah, as described in the Old Testament . He argues that while Catholicism's use of the "P" and "X" symbol is problematic, the menorah is a more authentic representation. He then reiterates his criticism of religious institutions for their "undesirable tricks" and their failure to prioritize humility and truth. He calls for a "Seonggol" mindset, urging people to embrace their true spiritual heritage rather than clinging to "Jingol" practices that lead to decline. He emphasizes that the Cheonbugyeong and Samil Singo are the roots of all national scriptures and must be understood to avoid spiritual "ruin."


## The Concept of Mushikmujong and Reincarnation 
The lecture introduces the concept of "Mushikmujong" (無始無終), meaning "no beginning, no end." This principle, central to the Cheonbugyeong, asserts that existence is a continuous cycle without a definitive start or conclusion. Huh Kyung young explains that what appears to be an end, such as death, is merely a transition to a new beginning. He likens it to a circle, where the end point immediately becomes the starting point. He states that the moment one closes their eyes in death is the moment they are reborn, often in a transformed state. He suggests that those who have lived virtuously may be reborn with improved physical attributes or in more desirable circumstances. He also claims that the experience of death is "fantastical" and "beautiful," so much so that those who truly experience it would wish to die again, implying that the current system prevents such a return.


## Investment in Rebirth and the Consequences of Actions 
Huh Kyung young emphasizes the importance of "investing" in one's rebirth through good deeds and spiritual understanding. He warns that neglecting this investment leads to undesirable reincarnations, such as being reborn as an insect or an animal. He uses the analogy of a bank default: just as a bank does not extend deadlines for overdue payments, there is no extension or negotiation in the process of rebirth. One's actions in life directly determine their next existence. He notes that animals, unlike humans, often retain memories of their past lives, experiencing psychological suffering as a result of their previous human actions. This "living hell" is a form of karmic retribution.


## The True Nature of the Sky and the Blindness of Humanity 
Huh Kyung young challenges the common perception of the sky's color. He asserts that the sky is fundamentally black, as indicated by ancient texts like the Cheonjamun ("Heaven is black, Earth is yellow"). The blue color we perceive is merely an illusion caused by the reflection of sunlight. He extends this metaphor to human perception, stating that just as diamonds do not shine in darkness without an external light source, people who lack spiritual understanding are "blind" to the truth, even when it is presented before them. He likens himself to a "diamond" that many fail to recognize, just as people failed to recognize Jesus. He describes the current era as a "night" of extreme capitalism, where material wealth is prioritized, leading to spiritual darkness.

To become "Seonggol," one must listen to Huh Kyung young's lectures, as those who do not remain "Jingol." He suggests that without this spiritual guidance, individuals risk being reborn into undesirable forms, such as pigs, and experiencing the regret of their past lives.


## The Three Extremes (Samgeuk) and the Three Truths (Samjin) 
Huh Kyung young explains the concept of "Samgeuk" (三極), representing Heaven, Earth, and Humanity. He clarifies that while Seonggol and Jingol belong to the "Earth" category, he himself belongs to "Heaven." He then introduces "Samjin" (三眞), which are the three truths:
1. **Mind (心, Shim):** Originates from Heaven (天極, Cheongeuk).
2. **Energy (氣, Gi):** Originates from Earth (地氣, Jigi).
3. **Body (身, Shin):** Originates from Humanity (人極, Ingeuk).

These three, Mind, Energy, and Body, form the "Samgeuk" and possess immense power, capable of influencing the entire world. He uses the examples of Hitler and the Japanese Emperor to illustrate how a single mind, divinely inspired (or possessed), can unleash destructive forces. He states that the Creator endowed each individual with free will and these three elements, making them capable of both creation and destruction.


## The Three Roots (Samgeun) and Three Paths (Samdo) 
Following Samjin, Huh Kyung young introduces "Samgeun" (三根), the three roots:
1. **Mind (心, Shim):** Produces character/nature (性品, Seongpum).
2. **Energy (氣, Gi):** Produces life/breath (命, Myeong).
3. **Body (身, Shin):** Produces essence/form (精, Jeong).

He then explains "Samdo" (三道), the three paths:
1. **Character (性品, Seongpum):** Leads to feeling/sensation (感, Gam).
2. **Life (命, Myeong):** Leads to respiration/breath (息, Sik).
3. **Essence (精, Jeong):** Leads to touch/contact (觸, Chok).

These three paths, particularly "Gam" (feeling), are linked to quantum mechanics.


## Quantum Mechanics, DNA, and Ancestral Influence 
Huh Kyung young connects the concept of "Gam" (feeling) to quantum mechanics and DNA. He explains that DNA, which he equates to "Pungsu" (풍수, geomancy) in ancient Korean thought, acts as a quantum link between individuals, particularly within families. He gives the example of a mother instinctively knowing when her distant child is in danger, attributing this to the quantum entanglement of their DNA. He asserts that the DNA in a parent's bones continues to interact with their children's DNA, even across vast distances. This quantum connection means that the state of ancestral graves (묘자리) can influence the well-being of descendants. He claims that he alone possesses the ability to communicate with and alter the quantum state of DNA, a feat impossible for scientists. He emphasizes that humans are predominantly "Jingol," with 95% divine influence and 5% human, meaning their "Gam" is inherently connected to Heaven.


## The Eighteen Realms (Sip Pal Gyeong) and the Three Prohibitions (Samgeum) 
The lecture presents a diagram illustrating how the three extremes (Samgeuk) are analyzed into the three truths (Samjin), three roots (Samgeun), and three paths (Samdo), ultimately leading to the "Eighteen Realms" (18 Gyeong). These realms encompass various human emotions and sensations, such as joy, fear, sorrow, anger, greed, and worry, as well as sensory experiences like sight, sound, smell, taste, and touch. To overcome the suffering arising from these realms, Huh Kyung young introduces the "Three Prohibitions" (Samgeum), which are essential for spiritual enlightenment:
1. **Jigam (止感):** Ceasing emotions. This involves controlling all feelings, whether positive or negative, to achieve a state of unhinderedness (Sasamooae).
2. **Josik (調息):** Regulating breath. This means maintaining calm and stable breathing, which in turn stabilizes the mind and prevents impulsive actions.
3. **Geumchok (禁觸):** Prohibiting touch/contact. This refers to controlling physical desires and attachments, preventing entanglement in worldly pleasures.

He states that mastering these three prohibitions leads to complete spiritual enlightenment and deification.


## The Creation of Humanity and the Limits of Science 
Huh Kyung young explains the creation of humanity through the principle of "Iljeokshigeo Mugyehasang" (一積十鉅無界河上), which describes how a single sperm develops into a complex human being within the womb. He emphasizes that this process, while understood scientifically in terms of sperm and egg, remains a profound mystery in its underlying cosmic principles. He asserts that scientists can observe the process but cannot create a seed or fully comprehend the fundamental "why" behind life's creation. He claims that the Cheonbugyeong, created 10,000 years ago, contains these profound universal principles, placing it above "Jingol" scriptures and positioning it as a "Seonggol" text.


## The Three Prohibitions (Samgeum) and Their Application 
Huh Kyung young elaborates on the practical application of the Three Prohibitions:

- **Jigam (止感):** Controlling emotions. He gives the example of a married man being tempted by an attractive neighbor, stating that Jigam would prevent him from succumbing to such desires. He also advises against uncontrolled anger in family disputes, as it can lead to children seeking to escape the home.
- **Josik (調息):** Regulating breath. This is about maintaining inner peace and stability, preventing anxiety and impulsive behavior.
- **Geumchok (禁觸):** Prohibiting touch/contact. This involves controlling physical desires and attachments, such as the urge to touch something or someone appealing.
He states that by mastering these three, one can overcome all misfortune. He also notes that the human mind is not singular but comprises nine different states, which manifest in eighteen forms, all of which can be managed through the Three Prohibitions.

In the context of the Cheonbugyeong, the circle symbolizes Heaven, the square symbolizes Earth, and the triangle symbolizes Humanity, all interconnected within a single entity. He also notes that the number 7 represents a higher dimension (4th dimension) compared to 5 (3rd dimension), indicating the elevated nature of the Cheonbugyeong's teachings.


## Il Manwangmanrae Yongbyeon Budongbon and Cheongol 
Huh Kyung young introduces the phrase "Il Manwangmanrae Yongbyeon Budongbon" (一萬往萬來用變不動本), meaning "one goes and comes ten thousand times, changing but not altering its fundamental nature." He interprets "Il" (one) as referring to himself, a being who appears in this world with infinite transformations but whose fundamental essence, originating from Heaven, remains unchanged. He distinguishes himself as "Cheongol" (天骨, heavenly bone), a higher category than Seonggol or Jingol, implying that he can alter human DNA, whereas humans cannot alter his or each other's. He reiterates the quantum connection of DNA, explaining that the state of ancestral remains influences descendants, and that this is the essence of quantum mechanics.

## The Nature of Prayer and the Three Minds 
Huh Kyung young criticizes conventional prayer, particularly "Jingol" prayers, which focus on present-day desires (e.g., praying for a child to pass an exam). He argues that such prayers are inherently "evil" because they implicitly wish for others to fail. He advocates for "Seonggol" prayers, which are not about asking for specific outcomes but rather about aligning with the future and trusting in the natural order. He then distinguishes between three types of "minds" and "suns":

- **True Mind (眞心, Jinsim):** Human sincerity, which he claims cannot move others.
- **Original Mind (本心, Bonsim):** The original, fundamental mind, which can move people.
- **Heavenly Mind (天心, Cheonsim):** The divine mind, which also has the power to move people.
Similarly, he identifies three types of "suns":

- **Original Sun (本太陽, Bontae-yang):** The sun itself, representing God.
- **Heavenly Sun (天太陽, Cheontae-yang):** The visible sun in the sky, providing light.
- **True Sun (眞太陽, Jintae-yang):** The sun that benefits humanity by nurturing crops and providing warmth.
He emphasizes that these distinctions are crucial for understanding the deeper cosmic principles.


## The Three Poisons (Samdok) and the Three Learnings (Samhak) 
Huh Kyung young connects the "Three Prohibitions" (Samgeum) to Buddhist concepts. He explains that the "Three Poisons" (三毒, Samdok) in Buddhism—greed (貪, Tam), anger (瞋, Jin), and ignorance (癡, Chi)—are the root causes of suffering. These three poisons manifest through the "Three Actions" (三業, Sam-eop): actions of the body, speech, and mind. The Three Prohibitions (Jigam, Josik, Geumchok) directly address and counteract these three actions, preventing the accumulation of negative karma. He then compares the Three Prohibitions to the "Three Learnings" (三學, Samhak) in Buddhism:
1. **Precepts (戒, Gye):** Managing the physical body.
2. **Meditation (定, Jeong):** Managing the mind through mental stability.
3. **Wisdom (慧, Hye):** Managing the spirit/soul through spiritual insight.

He asserts that these Buddhist "Three Learnings" are equivalent to the Cheonbugyeong's "Three Prohibitions," but he positions the Cheonbugyeong as a higher-dimensional "Seonggol" teaching compared to Buddhism's "Jingol" status.
