## The Truth of Three Studies and Profligacy


## Date: September 20, 2014


## Keywords: Three Studies, Profligacy, Time, Happiness, Prayer


## Content


## The Concept of Three Studies (Samhak) and Profligacy (Heorangbangtang) 
Huh Kyung young introduces the lecture by discussing the concepts of "Three Studies" (Samhak) and "Profligacy" (Heorangbangtang). He explains that "Heorangbangtang" refers to a state of emptiness and dissipation, symbolized by the characters for "empty" (虛) and "shedding tears" (浪). This term describes how people often spend their time without engaging in meaningful study or self-improvement. He emphasizes that time, as a human construct, does not inherently exist in the universe, yet people often squander it. 


## The Consequences of Profligacy 
Huh Kyung young warns that those who indulge in profligacy will find their spiritual status diminished in the afterlife. He asserts that true advancement comes from diligent study and self-cultivation during one's earthly life. He states that merely attending religious services or performing rituals like <em>cheon-do-jae</em> (a Korean Buddhist ritual for the deceased) is ineffective if one has spent their life in dissipation. 


## The Illusion of Time and the Purpose of Earthly Life 
Time is presented as a human-made illusion, non-existent in the cosmos. Huh Kyung young illustrates this by noting how past encounters with him feel as if they happened just yesterday, despite years having passed. He explains that humans are on Earth not to simply exist in their physical bodies, but to cultivate their spirit. The physical body is a temporary vessel for this spiritual learning, and without it, such study is impossible. 


## Happiness and Suffering: A Disguised Blessing 
Happiness, according to Huh Kyung young, often arrives disguised as suffering or hardship. He posits that those who bring true happiness may appear as beggars or unusual individuals. If one dismisses or treats such individuals poorly, they risk missing out on the happiness they could have received. 


## The Story of Rockefeller's Mother and the Furniture Store Owner 
Huh Kyung young recounts a story about Rockefeller's mother, who, while waiting for her son, sought shelter from the rain at a furniture store. The owner, despite her humble appearance, treated her with exceptional kindness, inviting her in, offering warmth, and even an umbrella. Impressed by his genuine compassion, Rockefeller's mother later instructed her son to entrust all of the Rockefeller Group's furniture needs to this man, leading him to become the chairman of a global furniture company. This anecdote illustrates that true blessings often come from unexpected sources and through acts of selfless kindness. 


## The Nature of Blessings and Misfortune 
Huh Kyung young emphasizes that blessings are often brought by humble or unusual individuals, not by those who are outwardly attractive or powerful. Conversely, he warns that associating with outwardly appealing but morally corrupt individuals can lead to misfortune and the downfall of one's household. He concludes that happiness requires "asceticism," which he equates with diligent study and self-cultivation. 


## The Importance of Recognizing Others 
True study involves learning not to disrespect anyone, regardless of their appearance or status. Huh Kyung young criticizes those who only flatter the wealthy and disregard the weak. He shares a Buddhist tale where a monk, who rudely dismissed a beggar, later realized the beggar was a Bodhisattva, highlighting the folly of judging others based on superficial qualities. This signifies that genuine learning prevents one from making such mistakes. 


## Prayer as Action: Benevolent Deeds 
Huh Kyung young redefines prayer not as a mere request for wishes, but as "action," specifically "benevolent action." He argues that praying for selfish desires is futile, as divine beings do not grant such prayers. Instead, true prayer involves performing good deeds, especially towards those who are unkind or difficult. He illustrates this with a story of a surgeon who was verbally abused by a patient before surgery, emphasizing the importance of treating everyone with kindness, as one never knows how circumstances might intertwine. 


## The Three Stages of Study: Mind, Spirit, and Soul 
Huh Kyung young outlines the "Three Studies" (Samhak) as a three-stage process involving the mind (마음), spirit (정신), and soul (영혼). He explains that while the spirit can be used for both good and evil, and the mind's transgressions are lighter, sins committed with the soul are viewed differently. The soul, from its perspective, transcends good and evil, focusing on the long-term implications of actions, especially concerning one's descendants and the afterlife. This higher perspective encourages empathy and understanding towards others. 


## The Three Elements of Philosophy: Emptiness, Harmony, and Rectitude (공, 원, 정) 
Huh Kyung young introduces three core philosophical elements:

- **Emptiness (空, Gong):** All things eventually return to emptiness. This understanding highlights the preciousness of the present moment and encourages kindness, as everything is transient. 
- **Harmony (圓, Won):** Striving for perfect harmony in all interactions. This means that the stronger party should yield in conflicts, such as a man yielding to a woman, or a ruling party listening to the opposition. 
- **Rectitude (正, Jeong):** All actions must be in accordance with the heavens. The character for "rectitude" (正) is broken down to illustrate that one's efforts should reach the heavens, signifying divine approval. Actions that do not align with this principle are considered "earthly" and lack true rectitude. 

## The Three Elements of Religion and Philosophy: Precepts, Concentration, and Wisdom (계, 정, 혜) 
Huh Kyung young connects these philosophical elements to the three core elements of religion:

- **Precepts (戒, Gye):** Personal self-discipline and adherence to moral guidelines. This is a personal endeavor, not to be imposed on others. 
- **Concentration (定, Jeong):** The spirit guarding the mind. This involves maintaining mental focus and preventing the mind from wandering or succumbing to negative impulses. 
- **Wisdom (慧, Hye):** The soul guarding the spirit. This is the highest stage, where one's actions are guided by profound spiritual insight and understanding. 
He explains that these three elements are interconnected and represent a progression towards spiritual enlightenment. When one reaches the stage of wisdom, they achieve a state of "graduation" and their endeavors become successful. 


## The Evolution of Religion: From Collective to Individual 
Huh Kyung young discusses the historical shift in religious practice, from communal gatherings at "common wells" (churches/temples) to individual spiritual cultivation. He notes that in ancient times, religious texts were exclusive to institutions, but with modern advancements like the internet and widespread access to information, individuals can now pursue spiritual understanding independently. He advocates for a future where personal enlightenment through the "Three Studies" replaces reliance on collective religious institutions, which he believes can lead to societal problems. He emphasizes that ultimately, each individual is responsible for their own salvation. 


## Prayer as Everyday Conduct and Expression 
Prayer is further defined as one's daily conduct and interactions with others. The way one speaks, the expression on their face, and their overall demeanor constitute their prayer. A kind and benevolent attitude is a positive prayer, while a hostile or critical one is a negative prayer that invites misfortune. He criticizes those who engage in negative behavior towards others but then seek blessings through formal prayers, calling it a contradiction that incurs further divine displeasure. 


## The Emille Bell and the Coming of the Messiah 
Huh Kyung young delves into the story of the Emille Bell, explaining that its unique sound, interpreted as "Emille" (엄마, "Mommy"), represents the cry of a child for its mother. He connects this to a classical poem, "Bonghwangjeong," which laments a world without hope and anticipates the arrival of a Messiah, symbolized by the phoenix (봉황). He interprets the "Emille" sound and the poem's imagery as a yearning for a savior, a "messenger" (메신저) or "Messiah" (메시아), who will bring hope and transform the world. 


## The Prophetic Vision of Yi Hang-bok 
Huh Kyung young highlights the significance of the poem "Bonghwangjeong," written by Yi Hang-bok, a highly esteemed official who held numerous high-ranking positions during the Joseon Dynasty. He suggests that Yi Hang-bok, 400 years ago, possessed a prophetic vision of the future, foreseeing a time of despair and the eventual arrival of a Messiah. Huh Kyung young implies that his own appearance in Korea is not coincidental but rather a fulfillment of this prophecy, signaling a time for radical political and societal change. 


## The Interplay of Mind, Spirit, and Soul in Mastery 
Huh Kyung young clarifies the roles of mind, spirit, and soul in achieving mastery and profound impact. He states that the mind is for managing the physical body, but true love and artistic expression come from the soul. He uses the examples of Beethoven's piano playing and a golf champion's swing, asserting that their greatness stems from their soul, not merely their mind. The mind, being fickle, needs to be managed by the spirit, and the spirit, in turn, by the soul, which operates on a higher plane of understanding. 


## The Roots of Suffering: Ignorance, Attachment, and Fear 
Huh Kyung young identifies three primary sources of human suffering (번뇌):

- **Ignorance (無明, Mumyeong):** A lack of understanding of the "Three Studies" and the true nature of reality. 
- **Attachment (渴愛, Garae):** Excessive clinging to worldly desires and relationships. 
- **Fear (恐怖, Gongpo):** Unnecessary anxieties and worries about future events. He asserts that overcoming these three roots of suffering requires diligent practice of the "Three Studies." 


## Re-evaluating Biblical Figures: Joseph and Abraham 
Huh Kyung young challenges conventional interpretations of biblical figures, viewing them through the lens of the "Three Studies."

- **Joseph:** Despite becoming a prime minister, Joseph is deemed "unfilial" for not immediately contacting his father, Jacob, who believed him dead, for nine years. From the soul's perspective, this neglect of his father's suffering is a significant failing. 
- **Abraham:** Abraham, considered the "father of faith," is criticized for twice selling his wife to kings for personal gain. While this might be seen as a pragmatic act from a worldly perspective, from the soul's perspective, it represents a profound moral compromise and "profligacy." Huh Kyung young argues that these examples demonstrate how even revered figures can fall into "profligacy" if they lack the wisdom of the "Three Studies." 


## The Distinction Between Good and Evil 
Huh Kyung young offers a unique definition of good and evil:

- **Evil (惡, Ak):** Applies only to oneself, serving as a constant reminder to guard against one's own potential wrongdoings. 
- **Good (善, Seon):** Applies to others, meaning one should always view others' actions benevolently and refrain from judgment. He criticizes the common human tendency to reverse this, judging others harshly while excusing one's own faults, which he believes leads to societal and personal ruin. 


## The Success of "The Admiral: Roaring Currents" (Myeongnyang) 
Huh Kyung young analyzes the success of the film "The Admiral: Roaring Currents," attributing it to its inversion of the typical narrative of suffering and loss. Unlike previous films where Admiral Yi Sun-sin died, this film portrays his victory and survival, offering a sense of hope and catharsis to the audience. He suggests that the film resonated with the public's subconscious desire for compensation and justice, especially in the wake of tragedies like the Sewol Ferry disaster. The Japanese invaders in the film are seen as symbolic representations of corrupt politicians and officials, whose defeat provides a release for public frustration. 


## The Historical Economic Dominance of Asia and the Soul in Craftsmanship 
Huh Kyung young highlights Asia's historical economic dominance, particularly Ming China, which accounted for 70% of global production. He notes that European nations were relatively poor until the Industrial Revolution. He emphasizes that Asian craftsmanship, especially in ceramics, possessed a "soul" that European imitations lacked. This "soul" refers to the dedication, intention, and spiritual essence infused into the creation, making Asian artifacts invaluable. 


## The Etymology of "China" and the Danger of Money 
He explains that the name "China" is derived from the Qin Dynasty (Chin), whose ceramics were the first to reach Europe, leading "china" to become synonymous with porcelain in English. He then draws a parallel between money ("don" in Korean) and dangerous objects like dynamite and diamonds, noting that words with the "d" sound in English often signify danger. He warns that possessing money without the wisdom of the "Three Studies" is like holding dynamite—it can lead to self-destruction through profligacy. 


## The Era of Materialism and the Decline of Spiritual Values 
Huh Kyung young describes the current era as one dominated by materialism, symbolized by the "mother" or "female" principle (음, Eum), where women's influence is rising. He argues that this excessive focus on material wealth, at the expense of spiritual values and family, is leading society to a critical "tipping point" (임계점), on the verge of collapse. He points to declining birthrates and an aging population as evidence of this impending crisis, emphasizing that society is prioritizing money and pleasure over the cultivation of the "Three Studies" and the spiritual "soul" (해, Hye). 


## The Importance of Balance and the Role of Government 
Huh Kyung young stresses the need for balance between the "female" (material) and "male" (spiritual) principles, advocating for a harmonious "middle ground" (사이 간, Sai Gan). He proposes government policies, such as marriage subsidies and full-time housewife allowances, to encourage childbirth and strengthen families, thereby addressing the demographic crisis and restoring societal balance. He warns that without such interventions, Korea faces a future of declining population and national weakness. 


## Concluding Remarks and Future Lectures 
Huh Kyung young concludes the lecture by promising to continue discussions on these profound topics in future sessions, encouraging attendees to bring more people to share in the "truth."
