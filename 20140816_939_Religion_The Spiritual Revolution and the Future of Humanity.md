## Content


## The Verticality of Catholicism and the Veil 
Huh Kyung young discusses the structural differences between Catholicism and other religions. He notes that Catholicism is uniquely structured with a vertical hierarchy, culminating in the Pope, whose authority extends globally. This contrasts with other religions, which he describes as having a more horizontal structure. He also points out the traditional restrictions on women within Catholicism, particularly the requirement for women to wear a veil when entering a church. This practice, he explains, is rooted in the belief that women are inherently sinful and must cover their heads so that God does not perceive them as female. He suggests that this tradition reflects a patriarchal view where men and women exist in a vertical relationship, with women created for men.


## The Spiritual Revolution of Pope Francis and the Veil 
Huh Kyung young acknowledges Pope Francis's efforts towards a global revolution, yet he criticizes the continued enforcement of the veil for women in Catholic churches. He argues that this practice is outdated and does not align with the progressive spirit of the 21st century, which should advocate for gender equality. He questions the logic behind requiring women to cover their heads to conceal their gender from God, especially when figures like the Virgin Mary are revered. He emphasizes that true spiritual progress involves recognizing women as equal to men, rather than perpetuating practices that diminish their status. He asserts that a genuine spiritual leader would challenge such traditions, promoting a more inclusive and equitable approach to faith.


## The Suppression of Korean Indigenous Religions 
Huh Kyung young highlights the historical suppression of indigenous Korean religions, particularly during the Joseon Dynasty and the Japanese colonial period. He notes that during the Joseon era, various religions, including Buddhism and Confucianism, had millions of followers. However, he claims that Japanese colonizers deliberately sought to eradicate these native spiritual traditions, viewing them as obstacles to their control over the Korean Peninsula. Religious leaders were persecuted, often labeled as independence activists, and many were killed. This suppression, he argues, created a vacuum that allowed foreign religions like Christianity and Japanese Buddhism to expand their influence in Korea. He laments that this historical context is often overlooked, leading to a spiritual subservience to foreign ideologies.


## The Concept of the "Heavenly Race" and Cultural Identity 
Huh Kyung young introduces the concept of Koreans as a "heavenly race" or a "bird race," distinct from other cultures. He explains that figures like Kang Jeungsan, who claimed to be from heaven, recognized Korea as a spiritual center, inhabited by a "yellow race" positioned between black and white races. He contrasts Korea's "phoenix culture" with China's "dragon culture," asserting that ancient Chinese history originally featured the phoenix, which was later replaced by the dragon to differentiate themselves from Korea. He emphasizes that Koreans, as a people born from an egg (symbolized by the phoenix), possess a unique spiritual heritage. He argues that this distinct cultural identity should be embraced, rather than blindly adopting foreign spiritual or political ideologies.


## The Vision for a World Unified Government 
Huh Kyung young outlines his vision for a unified world government, contrasting it with the current structure of the United Nations. He argues that the UN, with each member state retaining its military power, is inherently incapable of preventing war. His proposed world federal government would disarm individual nations, establishing a single federal army to maintain global peace. This system would allow for the swift intervention of a world federal court to address any nation or leader that threatens global stability. He envisions a world where national armies are abolished, and a unified defense force protects humanity from both internal conflicts and potential external threats. This, he believes, is the only way to achieve true global peace and prevent the ongoing suffering caused by conflicts.


## The Power of the Name "Huh Kyung young" 
Huh Kyung young demonstrates the unique spiritual power associated with his name. Through a series of physical tests, he illustrates that invoking his name can significantly enhance a person's physical strength and energy, making them resistant to external force. He contrasts this with invoking the names of other revered figures like Jesus, Buddha, or even one's own parents, which he claims do not produce the same effect. He explains that calling his name activates a cosmic energy that transforms the body's cells, making them stronger and more resilient. This phenomenon, he asserts, is a direct manifestation of his divine origin and his ability to channel universal energy. He suggests that this power can even reverse the aging process and heal ailments.


## The Importance of the Housewife Allowance 
Huh Kyung young advocates for a comprehensive housewife allowance, arguing that it is crucial for national stability and family well-being. He proposes providing a monthly stipend to women who choose to stay at home to raise children, emphasizing that this support would reduce the financial burden on families and prevent societal issues like divorce and crime. He contends that the primary educator of a child is the mother, and by empowering mothers to focus on their families, society as a whole benefits. He also suggests that this allowance would reduce the need for women to work long hours, which often leads to stress and health problems. He asserts that the funds for this allowance would come from reducing government waste, such as cutting parliamentary salaries and expenses.


## The Dynamic Nature of Physiognomy 
Huh Kyung young explains that a person's physiognomy (face reading) is not static but changes constantly based on their actions and interactions. He illustrates this with an anecdote about a man whose fortune changed after he saved a colony of ants from a flood. This act of kindness, he explains, altered the man's "face" or destiny, leading to a positive outcome. He emphasizes that judging others based on their appearance is misguided because their inner state and actions continuously reshape their outward manifestation. He concludes that all successes and failures, joys and sorrows, ultimately stem from one's own choices and deeds.
