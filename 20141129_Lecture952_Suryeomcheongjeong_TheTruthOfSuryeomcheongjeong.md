

## 20141129_Lecture952_Suryeomcheongjeong_TheTruthOfSuryeomcheongjeong

## Content


## The Concept of Suryeomcheongjeong and Divine Intervention 
Suryeomcheongjeong refers to a historical practice where a regent, often a queen dowager, governs from behind a screen while a young emperor is on the throne. Huh Kyung young asserts that this concept is now being applied on a global scale, but with a divine purpose. He states that the heavens, observing humanity's current state, have determined that intervention is necessary, leading to his arrival to implement a form of "Suryeomcheongjeong." He envisions a future where he will unify all global leaders and preside over them in this capacity.


## The Divine Test: Abraham's Sacrifice and Human Ignorance 
Huh Kyung young illustrates the divine perspective through the biblical story of Abraham. He explains that God commanded Abraham to sacrifice his son, not because God was unaware of Abraham's heart, but to test his resolve. The heavens, he claims, do not inherently know the depths of human hearts; they observe actions. Abraham's willingness to raise the knife proved his faith, after which God intervened. This narrative underscores the idea that divine judgment is based on demonstrated action, not mere intention, and that even individuals from humble or idolatrous backgrounds can be chosen by heaven after passing such trials.


## The Peril of Language: "Guillotine" and "Maha-Management" 
Huh Kyung young criticizes the use of certain powerful words by political figures, citing examples from South Korean politics. He recounts predicting turmoil in the Blue House after President Park Geun-hye's administration used the term "guillotine" for policy reforms, suggesting it indicated internal strife. Similarly, he foresaw Lee Kun-hee's collapse shortly after the Samsung chairman used the term "Maha-Management," a word he considers too profound for human use, implying it invoked a karmic consequence. He emphasizes that such potent language, typically reserved for divine beings, can bring about national misfortune when used carelessly by leaders, highlighting the importance of linguistic prudence in governance.


## The Misinterpretation of Buddhist Principles: Four Foundations of Mindfulness (Satipaṭṭhāna) 
Huh Kyung young delves into Buddhist philosophy, specifically the Four Foundations of Mindfulness (Satipaṭṭhāna). He explains that these involve observing the body by the body, the mind by the mind, thoughts by thoughts, and phenomena (Dharma) by phenomena. He clarifies that "Dharma" in this context refers to all visible phenomena in the world, not just legal statutes. For instance, observing one's body climbing stairs reveals its condition, and observing one's mind reveals its state. This practice encourages a direct, unmediated perception of reality.


## The Impermanence of All Phenomena (Sarva-dharma-anitya) 
Expanding on the concept of Dharma, Huh Kyung young emphasizes the Buddhist principle of impermanence (Sarva-dharma-anitya). He states that everything visible in the world, including physical objects like a pagoda or even human beings, is transient and will eventually turn to dust. This impermanence means that nothing truly remains, and what appears to exist is merely a temporary manifestation.


## Observing Reality as It Is (Yathābhūta-darśana) 
Huh Kyung young advocates for observing reality "as it is" (Yathābhūta-darśana). He provides an example: if a spouse yells, one should perceive them as a transient being who will eventually become dust, rather than reacting with anger. This perspective encourages detachment and understanding, recognizing that others' actions stem from their own internal states. By observing without judgment, one can avoid conflict and maintain inner peace.


## Critique of Buddhist Teachings: "All Dharmas Return to One" and Individual Karma 
Huh Kyung young critiques certain interpretations of Buddhist teachings, particularly the phrase "all dharmas return to one" (만법귀일, <em>manbeopgwiil</em>). He argues that while the idea of universal unity is understandable, it is a misrepresentation to suggest that all individuals are ultimately one. He asserts that each person's karma is unique and meticulously recorded by heaven, implying that individual accountability cannot be dissolved into a collective oneness. He contrasts this with Western thought, which clearly distinguishes between "I" and "you," fostering a more defined sense of individual responsibility and love. He also challenges the concept of "no-self" (무아, <em>mua</em>), arguing that the individual "self" never truly disappears, and that each person's life circumstances are a direct result of their unique past actions.


## The Prophetic Nature of Huh Kyung young's Policies 
Huh Kyung young highlights the prophetic nature of his past policy proposals. He cites a newspaper article detailing how many of his seemingly outlandish campaign promises from two decades ago are now being implemented or seriously considered by the government and other political parties. These include:

- **Marriage and Childbirth Incentives:** Providing 100 million won (50 million won each to the bride and groom) upon marriage and 30 million won upon childbirth.
- **Elderly Welfare:** Monthly payments of 700,000 won to seniors over 60.
- **Public Utility Subsidies:** 30,000 won per month for public utilities.
- **Debt Relief:** 20-year interest-free loans for credit defaulters.
- **Government Reform:** Reducing the number of National Assembly members to 100, abolishing local council member salaries, and eliminating local government elections.
- **Economic Revitalization:** Recovering 900 trillion won in underground funds through currency reform, activating farmland transactions, and 100% coverage for Sunday insurance.
- **Social Reforms:** Abolishing divorce records, implementing a volunteer military system, and providing 1 million won monthly support for small and medium-sized enterprise employees.
He claims that these policies, initially dismissed as absurd, are now being adopted because their necessity has become apparent, particularly in addressing issues like low birth rates and economic stagnation. He criticizes the government's current approach to housing and debt, arguing that their policies are akin to "drinking saltwater when thirsty," ultimately leading to national ruin. He proposes a radical currency reform to unearth 900 trillion won in underground funds, which would then be used to provide interest-free loans for middle-class citizens, thereby revitalizing the economy. He emphasizes that his welfare policies are "selective welfare," targeting the middle class and below, not the wealthy. He foresees a future where, as the nation faces decline, people will turn to his policies and recognize him as a visionary leader.


## The Illusion of Wealth in Capitalism 
Huh Kyung young argues that capitalism, despite its appearance, is a more scientific form of communism, where the state ultimately owns all wealth. He explains that even a billionaire like Lee Kun-hee, despite possessing vast assets, is not truly wealthy because these assets are subject to taxes and eventual confiscation by the state. He illustrates this by noting that property taxes over 20 years can effectively transfer ownership to the state, and income from bank deposits is also taxed. He concludes that the wealthy in a capitalist system are constantly burdened by taxes and the need to manage their assets to avoid losing them, making them, in a sense, the most "miserable" individuals. He states that the true owner of wealth is the state, which collects taxes and then, according to him, mismanages these funds.


## The Earth as a "Field of Blessings" (Bokbatchi) 
Huh Kyung young redefines the Earth as a "field of blessings" (복밭, <em>bokbat</em>), or a "hell" in another sense, where individuals come to cultivate good karma. He explains that suffering and hardship on Earth provide opportunities to accumulate blessings by helping the needy, the sick, and the hungry. In contrast, heaven offers no such opportunities for "blessing cultivation." He criticizes modern religious practices that focus on "begging for blessings" (축복, <em>chukbok</em>) from a higher power, arguing that true blessings are earned through one's own actions and efforts in this "field of blessings." He asserts that individuals are born with inherent blessings and should focus on sowing seeds of good deeds rather than merely praying for them.


## The Nature of Cosmic Energy and Divine Power 
Huh Kyung young describes his unique ability to manipulate cosmic energy, which he calls "negative electrons" (음전자, <em>eumjeonja</em>). He claims this energy is significantly faster than light, capable of healing people remotely, and even transforming matter. He demonstrates this by infusing energy into specific numbers, making a person's arm unmovable when that number is called. He states that this energy can be projected across vast distances, even to other galaxies, and its speed can be amplified at will. He asserts that this power is divine, allowing him to influence objects, spaces, and individuals, and that this ability is why he has come to implement Suryeomcheongjeong.


## The Global Implications of Population and Economy 
Huh Kyung young discusses the critical role of population in national and global economics. He points out that the United States, with its relatively smaller population compared to China, struggles economically without engaging in conflicts to sell weapons. He argues that China's large population of 1.3 billion provides a robust internal market, making it economically self-sufficient without resorting to war. He concludes that for the Korean people to thrive globally, the population must increase significantly, and Koreans should expand their presence worldwide. He reiterates that his 20-year-old population policies, such as marriage and childbirth incentives, were crucial for national economic survival, a fact he believes is now becoming evident.
