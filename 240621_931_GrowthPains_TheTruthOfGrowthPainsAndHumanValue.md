## Content


## The Truth of Growth Pains and Human Value 
Huh Kyung young emphasizes the profound truth of "growth pains" and its impact on human value. He illustrates this with an anecdote about a private who was helped by a senior officer. Years later, the private, now a prosecutor, recognized the senior officer, who had forgotten him. This story highlights that those who have experienced hardship and growth pains tend to be more empathetic and respectful towards others. Conversely, individuals who have not endured such struggles often lack humility and respect, failing to greet elders or acknowledge the suffering of others. This phenomenon is likened to bamboo, which develops internal strength through segmented growth, rather than growing straight and hollow. Children raised without experiencing hardship are like hollow bamboo, prone to breaking under the slightest pressure when parental support is gone. 


## The Inversion and Distortion of Values 
Huh Kyung young asserts that contemporary society suffers from a complete inversion and distortion of values. This "inversion" (전도, <em>jeondo</em>) means values are turned upside down, while "distortion" (왜곡, <em>waegok</em>) implies a deliberate perversion of what is right, even when one knows better. For instance, children who intentionally disrespect elders or challenge parental authority, despite knowing it is wrong, exemplify this distortion. Such individuals stubbornly cling to incorrect beliefs, asserting them as right. Those who have not experienced growth pains are particularly prone to misunderstanding human value. 


## Rediscovering Value Through Adversity 
A poignant example of rediscovering true value is seen in the aftermath of the Sewol Ferry tragedy. Parents who once worried about their children's academic performance or frequently argued with them suddenly realized the immense value of their child's mere existence after witnessing the deaths of 304 other children. This tragic event brought them out of their "inversion and delusion" (전도몽상, <em>jeondo-mongsang</em>), making them grateful for their child's health and presence, regardless of academic achievement. This realization underscores that true human value becomes apparent when one experiences hardship and overcomes the delusions of inverted values. 


## The Absence of Growth Pains in Modern Youth 
Huh Kyung young observes that modern youth often lack growth pains, unlike previous generations, particularly those who lived through the Korean War and its aftermath. The older generation, having rebuilt the nation from ruins, commands automatic respect, regardless of their current status. This respect stems from their immense suffering and contributions. However, younger generations, who did not experience such hardships, often fail to appreciate the struggles of their elders, sometimes even questioning why they "suffered" when modern conveniences like instant noodles exist. This generational gap in understanding the value of hardship leads to a disconnect in appreciating human worth. 


## Subjective vs. Objective Value and Religious Freedom 
Human value can be categorized into subjective and objective values. Subjective value is based on an individual's self-perception and personal aspirations, while objective value is viewed from the perspective of a higher power, such as God or parents. Huh Kyung young then delves into the concept of religious freedom, referencing Article 20 of the Korean Constitution, which guarantees freedom of religion. He, as a top law graduate, emphasizes that this article ensures individuals can practice their religion freely without criticism. However, he highlights a critical misinterpretation of Article 20, Paragraph 2, which states that religion and politics must be separated. This separation, he argues, has been wrongly interpreted as preventing any interaction or mutual critique between religion and politics, leading to corruption in both spheres. 


## The Interdependence of Politics and Religion 
Huh Kyung young contends that the misinterpretation of the separation of church and state has led to a decline in both political and religious integrity. He believes that just as a husband and wife should constructively interfere in each other's lives for a longer, healthier relationship, so too should politics and religion engage in a degree of mutual oversight. A complete lack of friction, like a perfectly harmonious couple, can lead to stagnation and a shorter lifespan. A slight amount of stress or constructive conflict is beneficial for growth and longevity. Therefore, politics and religion should engage in a healthy dialogue, with each questioning the other's actions for the betterment of society. 


## The Kennedy Assassination and Religious Suppression 
Huh Kyung young cites the assassination of U.S. President John F. Kennedy as an example of the dangers of religious suppression. Kennedy, a Catholic in a predominantly Protestant nation, mandated the removal of prayer from public schools, arguing for religious freedom. This decision, according to Huh Kyung young, led to a significant increase in juvenile crime (70%), as children lost the moral grounding provided by daily prayer and reflection on their parents' sacrifices. He suggests that Kennedy's actions, perceived as an attack on the majority religion, created a backlash that ultimately led to his death. The subsequent immediate swearing-in of Vice President Lyndon B. Johnson in the hospital, with Kennedy's body nearby, further fueled speculation about a political and religious conspiracy. 


## The True Meaning of "Heaven Above and Heaven Below, Only I Am Honored" 
Huh Kyung young reinterprets the Buddhist saying "Heaven above and heaven below, only I am honored" (천상천하 유아독존, <em>cheonsangcheonha yu-a-dokjon</em>). He clarifies that this statement, often misunderstood as an assertion of individual superiority, actually signifies that no one exists alone and that every single human being is uniquely precious and worthy of respect. It means that in the vastness of existence, each person is as valuable as any other, and there is no one higher or lower. This concept emphasizes the interconnectedness of humanity and the inherent dignity of every individual, regardless of their number. 


## The Significance of "Everywhere is Buddha, Every Act is Worship" 
Building on the concept of inherent human value, Huh Kyung young connects it to the Buddhist principles of "everywhere is Buddha" (처처불상, <em>cheocheo bulsang</em>) and "every act is worship" (사사불공, <em>sasa bulgong</em>). This means that every person we encounter is a Buddha, and every action we take, from going to work to receiving education, is an act of worship or an offering to the divine. This perspective elevates the mundane, imbuing all aspects of life with spiritual significance and reinforcing the idea that every individual possesses immense value. 


## The Dangers of Subjective Judgment: The Forbidden Fruit of Good and Evil 
Huh Kyung young warns against the "forbidden fruit of good and evil" (선악과, <em>seonakgwa</em>), which he equates with judging others based on subjective values. He explains that humans are prone to "inversion and delusion" (전도몽상), where their thoughts are twisted, leading them to evaluate others based on superficial criteria like success or failure. This subjective judgment, he argues, is what the Bible refers to as eating the forbidden fruit. Only God, or Buddha in Buddhist terms, can truly know and judge the value of a person. When humans judge each other as "good" or "evil," they usurp a divine role, leading to a lack of love and compassion. 


## The Nine Fruits of the Holy Spirit and the Four Techniques of Love 
Huh Kyung young explains that love is manifested through "four techniques" and leads to the "nine fruits of the Holy Spirit."
1. **Facial Expression:** The first technique is to bring joy to others through a bright and cheerful facial expression. A smile can positively influence those around you, while a grim or suspicious look can create negative energy.
2. **Speech:** The second technique is to use kind and pleasant words. Responding graciously even when inconvenienced, such as having one's foot stepped on, spreads positivity.
3. **Material Giving:** The third technique involves giving material things, even small gestures like buying a parent their favorite snack. This act of selfless giving, even if it means personal sacrifice, fosters love and reciprocity.
4. **Physical Service:** The fourth technique is to offer physical help and service, such as assisting someone in a wheelchair or volunteering. These acts of service are considered worship and invite the presence of the Holy Spirit.

These four techniques, when practiced, cultivate the nine fruits of the Holy Spirit, which include joy, peace, patience, kindness, goodness, faithfulness, gentleness, and self-control. Those who judge others with the "forbidden fruit of good and evil" cannot possess these qualities and instead operate in opposition to them, leading to constant dissatisfaction and stress. 


## The Importance of Growth Pains for Leadership 
Huh Kyung young concludes by reiterating that individuals who have experienced growth pains and hardship are better equipped to understand and empathize with the public. Leaders who have struggled tend to be more attuned to the needs and sentiments of the people, leading to effective governance. Conversely, those from privileged backgrounds who have not faced adversity often fail to connect with the populace, leading to a disconnect and ultimately, a loss of public trust. He emphasizes that judging others is a divine prerogative, and humans should refrain from it to foster love and avoid unnecessary suffering.
