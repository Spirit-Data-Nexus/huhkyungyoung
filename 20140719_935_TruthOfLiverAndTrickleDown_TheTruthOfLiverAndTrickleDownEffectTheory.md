## The Philosophy of Prioritizing Duty 
Huh Kyung young recounts a childhood experience that shaped his philosophy of prioritizing duty over immediate gratification. Unlike other children who would play first and then reluctantly complete chores, he always finished his tasks, such as gathering firewood, before engaging in leisure. This approach, he explains, made work enjoyable and effortless, as he would rest after completing his duties, rather than being exhausted by them. He notes that despite his attempts to encourage others to adopt this method, they rarely did. This early habit instilled in him the importance of fulfilling responsibilities first, a principle he applies to all aspects of life.


## The Bond with Animals and the Essence of Love 
Huh Kyung young further illustrates his philosophy through his deep connection with animals, particularly a calf named Millim. He describes how he nurtured the calf, even sharing his meager barley rice with it when it refused to eat after being separated from its mother. This act of love fostered an extraordinary bond, allowing Millim to understand his calls from a distance, unlike other cattle. He spent countless hours with Millim, sleeping in the barn, cleaning its stall, and conversing with it, treating it as a family member. This profound relationship, he suggests, exemplifies the transformative power of love and genuine connection, contrasting it with the transactional approach of others who viewed animals merely as commodities. He recounts the painful experience of witnessing Millim's mother being sold for slaughter, an event that deeply traumatized him and led him to leave his hometown at the age of 14, feeling a profound sense of betrayal and the harsh reality of human cruelty.


## The Cycle of Reincarnation and the Importance of Good Deeds 
Huh Kyung young delves into the concept of reincarnation, asserting that the Christian idea of resurrection is merely a part of the cyclical process of rebirth. He challenges the notion of a mass resurrection, arguing that it would lead to an unsustainable population crisis. Instead, he posits that humans are continuously reborn, much like leaves on a tree. He emphasizes that merely living a good life, without actively contributing to the well-being of others or spreading truth, will lead to rebirth as an animal in a state of suffering. He urges individuals to actively seek opportunities to do good deeds daily, as these actions accumulate immense spiritual energy. This perspective underscores the importance of wisdom and proactive benevolence beyond simple adherence to societal norms.


## The Pain of Separation and the Value of Suffering 
Huh Kyung young reflects on the profound pain of separation, particularly his departure from his hometown after witnessing the slaughter of Millim's mother and visiting his own mother's grave. He describes the overwhelming grief that blurred his vision, making it seem as if he was abandoning a living person. He connects this personal suffering to a broader philosophical understanding of pain as a "preventive shot" in life. Just as vaccinations protect against disease, experiencing hardship in youth prepares individuals for future challenges, preventing them from succumbing to difficulties later in life. He argues that suffering should not be feared but embraced as a necessary part of growth, acknowledging that even a Messiah like Jesus experienced pain during his separation from his mother on the cross. He concludes that while the circumstances of suffering can be tragic, blaming others is unproductive; instead, one must confront the painful reality of human existence.


## Economic Theories: From Parasitic to Self-Reliant and Beyond 
Huh Kyung young critiques various economic theories and historical economic policies, starting with the "parasitic economy" of post-war Korea. He explains that during the era of the 4-H movement (Health, Head, Hands, Heart), Korea became dependent on foreign aid, particularly from the United States. This dependency led to the decline of domestic industries, such as goat farming and wheat cultivation, as free imports of milk and flour made local production unnecessary. He argues that this reliance fostered a culture of idleness and gambling, rather than genuine national development.

He then traces the transition to a "self-reliant economy" in the 1960s, driven by the establishment of independent industries like flour mills, cement factories (e.g., Mungyeong Cement), and sugar refineries (e.g., CheilJedang). This period marked a shift towards domestic production of essential goods. Following this, the economy progressed through stages of light industry (e.g., bicycles, wigs), heavy industry (e.g., automobiles, shipbuilding), petrochemicals, and advanced industries like semiconductors in the 1980s. The 2000s ushered in the era of the knowledge and information industry. Huh Kyung young asserts that a socialist approach would have stifled this progress, keeping Korea perpetually dependent.


## Understanding Inflation, Deflation, and Stagflation 
Huh Kyung young clarifies key economic terms:

- **Inflation:** Characterized by rising prices and a decrease in the value of money. He notes that wealthy individuals often desire inflation because it increases the value of their assets, such as real estate, even if it erodes the purchasing power of money.
- **Deflation:** The opposite of inflation, where prices fall, and the value of money increases.
- **Stagflation:** A combination of rising prices (inflation) and a stagnant or declining economy.
- **Agflation:** A specific type of stagflation where agricultural product prices and general commodity prices rise simultaneously.
He emphasizes that while a moderate level of inflation is necessary for economic growth, excessive inflation can lead to problems.


## Wealth Distribution and the Rise of Virtual Currency 
Huh Kyung young highlights the stark disparity in wealth distribution, noting that in the U.S., 1% of the population holds 37% of the wealth, while in Korea, the top 1% controls approximately 40%. This concentration of wealth, he argues, leads the elite to favor inflationary policies that benefit their asset values, often at the expense of the middle and lower classes.

He then introduces the concept of virtual currency, specifically Bitcoin, as a potential solution to the instability of traditional fiat currencies. Bitcoin, he explains, maintains a constant value without exchange rate fluctuations, making it attractive to those without significant assets who are vulnerable to currency depreciation. He predicts that the world is moving towards a global virtual currency system, as trust in national currencies and banking systems erodes due to economic instability and real estate bubbles. He proposes the creation of a "Bonjwa Coin" as a global currency to eliminate exchange rates, inflation, and economic discrimination, ultimately leading to a world without borders.


## The Trickle-Down Effect and its Limitations 
Huh Kyung young explains the "Trickle-Down Effect" (or Trickle-Down Economics) as a top-down economic policy where government funds are directly invested in large corporations (chaebols) like Samsung, Lucky, and Goldstar. These corporations, in turn, are expected to stimulate the economy by supporting small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) and small business owners. This "rainwater effect" assumes that wealth will naturally flow from the top to the bottom.

However, he argues that this theory is outdated. While it might have been necessary during Korea's early development under President Park Chung-hee to build foundational infrastructure and industries, it is no longer suitable for the current economic landscape. He advocates for a "Trickle-Up" policy, where economic growth is driven from the bottom up. He criticizes the current government for lacking innovative ideas and failing to implement such a policy, giving them a "zero score" for their economic strategy.


## Economic Self-Sufficiency and Public Health 
Huh Kyung young passionately advocates for national self-sufficiency in food and medicine. He asserts that Korea can produce 100% of the food currently imported from China, much of which is contaminated with pesticides. He suggests mobilizing the 5 million unemployed and elderly population to cultivate medicinal herbs and agricultural products in the country's abundant mountains, providing them with employment and contributing to national health.

He highlights the economic imbalance with China, where Korea exports $200 billion but imports only $80 billion, leading China to perceive Korea as dependent. Similarly, he points out Korea's persistent trade deficit with Japan, amounting to tens of trillions of won annually, which he equates to giving away years of national agricultural output.

He then connects these economic issues to public health, criticizing the government for allowing citizens, especially young women, to consume pesticide-laden Chinese food, preservative-filled foreign coffee, and unhealthy fats, while also being exposed to tobacco smoke. He condemns these practices as "treasonous" and vows to hold responsible officials accountable if he becomes president, emphasizing the devastating impact on the health and future of the younger generation. He points out that children of non-smoking parents in Korea are generally taller, have better skin, and are more attractive, but their potential is being undermined by contaminated food and unhealthy environments.


## The Concept of "Gan" (Seasoning/Balance) in Life and Governance 
Huh Kyung young introduces the concept of "Gan" (간), which literally means "liver" but metaphorically refers to "seasoning" or "balance" in the context of food, and by extension, the crucial element that determines the quality and acceptability of anything. He explains that just as a tiny amount of seasoning can make or break a dish, a small but critical element can determine the success or failure of an entire system, be it the economy, politics, or even human life.

He argues that if the "Gan" of the economy is wrong, the trickle-down effect or any other economic policy will fail to benefit the common people, leading to widespread unemployment, bankruptcies, and debt. He criticizes current economic policies that favor large corporations with tax exemptions, suggesting they lack proper "Gan" and are not palatable to the public.

Extending this metaphor to politics, he states that politicians who are out of touch with the common people's needs cannot properly "season" policies. He contrasts this with a leader who regularly visits markets and understands the public's pulse, thereby ensuring that policies have the right "Gan." He concludes that a "Gan-less" politics or economy is inherently flawed and unsustainable.


## The Spiritual Dimension of Healing and Human Endeavor 
Huh Kyung young distinguishes between "treatment" (치료, <em>chiryo</em>), which is human-led intervention using tools like medicine or surgery, and "healing" (치유, <em>chiyu</em>), which is divine intervention or the body's natural, God-given immunity. He asserts that while human efforts in treatment are important, a significant spiritual element (approximately 50%) influences the outcome. He illustrates this with the example of a doctor performing surgery: despite perfect technical execution, the patient's recovery can vary due to unseen spiritual factors, such as the timing of the surgery or the patient's overall spiritual state.

He further extends this to traditional practices like making soy sauce (<em>ganjang</em>), where even skilled artisans pray for its success, fearing that negative spiritual influences (e.g., family discord, a neighbor's misfortune) could spoil it. This highlights the belief that human endeavors are intertwined with spiritual forces. He advises individuals to accumulate good deeds to build spiritual "savings," which can positively influence these unseen factors, even in seemingly random events like car accidents. He emphasizes that relying solely on technical skill without considering the spiritual dimension is insufficient.


## The Scale of the Universe and the Human Body 
Huh Kyung young introduces a unique numerical scale, starting from "Eok" (10^8) and progressing through "Jo" (10^12), "Gyeong" (10^16), "Hae" (10^20), "Ja" (10^24), "Yang" (10^28), "Gu" (10^32), "Gan" (10^36), "Jeong" (10^40), and "Jae" (10^44). He notes that while humans typically operate within the "Eok" or "Jo" range of wealth, his own name, "Kyung young," contains the "Gyeong" unit, signifying a vastness beyond ordinary human comprehension. He explains that these immense numbers are essential for calculating cosmic phenomena and even for understanding the intricate details of DNA.

He then contrasts these large numbers with microscopic units:

- **Micro:** 10^-6 meters (one-millionth of a meter)
- **Nano:** 10^-9 meters (one-billionth of a meter)
- **Angstrom:** 10^-10 meters (one-ten-billionth of a meter)
- **Pico:** 10^-12 meters (one-trillionth of a meter)
- **Femto:** 10^-15 meters (one-quadrillionth of a meter)
- **Atto:** 10^-18 meters (one-quintillionth of a meter)

He states that while current science has reached the nano level, his spiritual power operates at the "Zepto" level, capable of modifying human cells at an incredibly minute scale. He asserts that the human body's genetic and cellular structure operates within the "Yang" unit (10^28), implying a profound complexity that requires an equally profound spiritual intervention to heal.
